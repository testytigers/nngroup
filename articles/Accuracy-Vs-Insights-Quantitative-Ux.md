<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Better to accept a wider margin of error in usability metrics than to spend the entire budget learning too few things with extreme precision.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Last week, I made a slide for the new <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/ux-basic-training/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for full-day training course"> User Experience (UX) Basic Training course</a> with the <strong> recommended number of test users </strong> for different types of studies. I like teaching foundational courses because they afford me just this kind of opportunity — to distill 25 years of usability process research into a single table. Patterns crystallize when complex topics are condensed to the essence.</p>

<p>For example, why do we recommend <a class="old" href="/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/" title="Alertbox: Card Sorting: How Many Users to Test?"> testing more users for card sorting</a> than for <a class="old" href="/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/" title="Alertbox: Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users "> usability studies</a>? Because the usual rule, <em> "we're testing the system, not you," </em> doesn't apply to card sorting. When eliciting <a class="old" href="/articles/mental-models/" title="Alertbox"> mental models</a>, we're actually testing the individual users instead of a predefined artifact, and the variability is thus larger.</p>

<p>The thing that surprised me most about my own table: I recommend doing most <a class="old" href="/articles/quantitative-studies-how-many-users/" title="Alertbox: Quantitative Studies: How Many Users to Test"> quantitative user testing with a sample size</a> that typically entails a <strong> 19% margin of error</strong>.</p>

<p>19% sounds sloppy. How come a fairly low level of accuracy usually suffices in estimating usability metrics?</p>

<p>Two reasons:</p>

<ul>

<li>A 19% confidence interval pretty much represents the worst-case outcome. Usually, the error is much smaller.</li>

<li>The <a class="old" href="/articles/website-usability-differences/" title="Alertbox: How Big is the Difference Between Websites?"> average usability difference between websites</a> is <strong> 64%</strong>, so even in those few cases where we get a 19% measurement error, we'd usually pick the correct winner anyway.</li>

</ul>

<p>These mathematical points suffice to defend the idea of saving budget and limiting quantitative studies to mid-sized samples.</p>

<p>But there are two deeper arguments that are even more important.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-focus-on-big-problems-1">Focus on Big Problems</h2>

<p>You <strong> shouldn't care about small issues </strong> in usability. At this stage, we still have bigger fish to fry. When redesigning a website for usability, the average <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-roi-declining-but-still-strong/" title="Alertbox: Usability ROI (Return on Investment)"> improvement in key performance indicators</a> (KPI) is <strong> 83%</strong>. Clearly, most websites still contain horrible usability problems. Intranets and mobile sites/apps are often even worse.</p>

<p>Your focus should thus be on the really big design problems, where your user experience is failing to meet customer needs. Typically, there are only a few <a class="old" href="/articles/10-high-profit-redesign-priorities/" title="Alertbox: 10 High-Profit Redesign Priorities"> issues with immense bottom-line impact</a>. Better to invest heavily in those crucial improvements than mess around with changes that'll gain you only a percent or two.</p>

<p>Wasting your budget on overly precise measurements can easily sidetrack you from the important issues; for sure, you'd have less budget left over to work on them.</p>

<p>Maybe in 20 years, user interfaces will be good enough that our only remaining goal will be to fine-tune them for the last few percents' quality gain. That's definitely not the case today.</p>
