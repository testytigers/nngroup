<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>App switching in iOS 9 can disorient users in multiple ways. Simple design fixes can significantly improve the user experience.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>When people use their phones to plan dinner out with friends on a Friday night, there is often a lot of back and forth between different apps — looking up restaurant ratings on an app such as Yelp, checking restaurant availability on OpenTable, and synchronizing with everybody in their party through email and SMS. Research recently published confirms that users who look for information on their mobile phones often interact with more applications and for longer — on average, they <strong>interact with 4.6 different apps</strong> and launch apps 10.8 times within the same information-seeking session (compared with only 2.6 apps and 3.9 launches per session in other types of sessions, such as browsing sessions).</p>

<p>So there is definitely a need to support mobile <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/multitasking-mobile/">multitasking</a> and combining multiple sources of information, or, at least, switching back and forth between multiple apps.  Besides going back to the home screen and launching a second recently used app by tapping on its icon, there are two quick ways to switch between apps:</p>

<ol>

<li>Go to the list of recent apps, find the app you’re looking for, and tap on it. (In iOS this is done by tapping the <em>Home </em>button twice; in Android by tapping the <em>Recent Apps </em>physical button.)</li>

<li>Use the <em>Back </em>button.</li>

</ol>

<p>Until iOS 9, the second option was only available to Android users: the phone’s physical <em>Back </em>button works across applications and thus allows users to go back to a previous app, provided that the current app was launched from the previous one. In other words, if someone sent you a link in an email and you tapped on it while reading the message in the Gmail app, the link would be launched in the browser and once you’ve finished reading the page you’d be able to go back to Gmail by tapping the phone’s <em>Back </em>button. However, if you decided to stay on that site and browse one more page, tapping the <em>Back</em> button would not take you back to the app, but to the page you’d seen before in the browser’s history. In other words, the Android physical <em>Back </em>button has a meaning that is fairly easy to understand by the users: it undoes the previous action.</p>

<p>With iOS 9, Apple introduced some changes to how users can switch between apps: not only did it redesign the list of recent apps, but it also introduced a new <em>Back-to-app </em>button.  The new list of recent apps displays the different apps in a stack of cards, not unlike the stack of cards used for the same purpose in Android. But Android makes better use of the limited screen space, by stacking cards vertically instead of horizontally and by using a fish-eye effect to still fit in some of the apps used farther away in the past.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="List of recent apps in Android and IOS 9" border="0" height="554" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/09/23/recent-apps.png" width="640"/>

<figcaption><em>List of recent apps: Android (left) uses a vertical stack of cards and iOS 9 (right) uses a horizontal stack of cards for the apps most recently run. The iOS list now looks like a horizontal version of the Android equivalent, with the previously used app displayed in larger detail than the other apps and the current app being the last one on the left.</em> </figcaption>

</figure>

<p>However, one of the most notable changes in iOS 9 is not the new list of apps, but rather the new <em>Back-to-app </em>button. Unlike the Android physical <em>Back </em>button, the iOS <em>Back-to-app </em> has a very limited functionality: In certain circumstances, it allows people to go back to the previous app they used.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-issues-with-the-back-to-app-button-1">Issues with the <em>Back-To-App </em>Button</h2>

<p>As usability experts, we are trained to look for and see the worst in interfaces. So let me preface my list of complaints by admitting right away that the <em>Back­-to-App </em>button is definitely useful since it decreases the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/">interaction cost</a> of switching apps. However, the implementation creates some potential issues. Luckily app designers can alleviate at least one of these problems.</p>

<ol>

<li><strong>Target size</strong>. First, it is obvious that the <em>Back-to-app </em>button is an afterthought — it feels almost like a Band-Aid on a beautifully finished product. Its size is tiny and its position at the very top of the screen makes it easy to accidentally hit other buttons that apps traditionally place in the top left corner of their design (for instance, the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/support-mobile-navigation/">hamburger menu</a> or the in-app <em>Back </em>button).

<p>Although is hardly noticeable because of the small size, its lack of discoverability is likely not a huge problem: since this is an operating system (and not a random app or website), users will have many opportunities to eventually notice it and hopefully learn to use it.</p>

<div style="text-align:center">

<figure class="caption" style="display:inline-block"><img alt="" border="0" height="569" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/09/23/2015-09-18-160154.png" width="320"/>

<figcaption><em> When Yelp is invoked directly from the App Store, The </em>Back to iTunes Store <em>button appears in the top left corner of the screen.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

</div>

</li>

<li><strong>Mental model. </strong> The <em>Back-to-app</em> button only takes users back to a previous app if the current app has been <strong>invoked</strong> by that previous app. In other words, if you’re listening to a song in Pandora, then double tap the phone’s <em>Home</em> button to invoke the list of recent apps, and finally select iTunes from that list to look for the same song, there won’t be a <em>Back to Pandora </em>button displayed in iTunes. However, if you tap a shopping-cart icon in Pandora and it takes you to iTunes directly, then the <em>Back to Pandora </em>button will be displayed.

<p> </p>

<p>Some users will have a hard time understanding precisely why the button sometimes appears up at the top of the screen and sometimes doesn’t. Because of this apparently nondeterministic behavior, people may end up not relying much on the button and opting instead to use the other, more beaten and reliable path, of switching apps  — namely, hitting the phone’s <em>Home</em> button twice to invoke the list of recent apps. The designer’s <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/">mental model</a> of the <em>Back-to-app </em>button is not easily conveyed to the user.</p>

<p>Something that always works is preferable to something that sometimes works. (Because if it always works, it’s a no-brainer to reach for it. But if it only works sometimes, there’s a risk in looking for the feature, only to be disappointed. People don’t like disappointments and, once disappointed, they may not reach again for the same feature.)</p>

<p>Moreover, the <em>Back-to-app </em> button is not recursive — you can only go back one level. So if you click on a link in Mail and it takes you to the App Store to download, say, IMDb, and then you open IMDb, you will only be able to navigate back to the App Store, but not back to the Mail app using the <em>Back-to-app</em>.</p>

<p>The only one level of <em>Back </em>may not seem like a big problem. After all, users’ <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/">working memory</a> is limited and they are unlikely to keep track of a long chain of apps that they’ve used over time. However, remember that users do use the <em>Back </em>button repeatedly on web browsers to retrace their steps until they return to a safe place, such as their browsers’ homepage.</p>

</li>

<li><strong>Conflict with the in-app <em>Back </em>button</strong>.If you land on a deep page within an app, often the top left corner of that screen is taken by the <em>Back </em>button. But what does the <em>Back </em>button mean when this is the first page you’re seeing in that app? In the Yelp example above, what would <em>Back </em>take you to  — the previous page you’ve visited in Yelp the last time you’ve used the app? Who can remember what that page was, especially if your last session wasn’t so recent?

<p> </p>

<p>Although that button in Yelp does take users to the previously visited page (in this case <em>Nearby)</em>, laudably, Yelp has likely realized very quickly the potential for confusion, since an update seems to have fixed this problem by labeling the button <em>Nearby </em>instead of <em>Back</em>.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Imdb Emmys page (left) and Yelp Voya restaurant page " border="0" height="535" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/09/23/yelp-and-imdb.png" width="640"/>

<figcaption><em>Left: IMDB’s </em>Back<em> button has a confusing meaning for users who come from a different app. Right: A quick update in Yelp fixed the problem of an ambiguous in-app </em>Back<em> button by labeling it </em>Nearby<em> to indicate that it takes users (back) to the </em>Nearby<em> page.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<div style="text-align:center">

<figure class="caption" style="display:inline-block"><img alt="Messenger page displaying conversations with one friend" border="0" height="569" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/09/23/messenger.png" width="320"/>

<figcaption><em>When the Messenger app is invoked from Facebook, the button displayed in the top left corner correctly reads </em>Recent<em> to point to a screen one level up in the information hierarchy.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

</div>

<p>From the three issues pointed out here, this is the only one that can actually be fixed by app designers. And the fix is an old design recommendation: <strong>do not call a button <em>Back </em>unless it undoes the previous action taken by the user. </strong> Like it or not, this is the behavior of <em>Back </em>that users have come to expect from their experience with the browser <em>Back </em>button on the desktop. If there is a circumstance in which the <em>Back </em>button does not undo the last action taken by users, then increase the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/">information scent</a> by labeling that button with the name of the page that it leads to.</p>

</li>

</ol>

<h2 id="toc-a-note-on-apps-invoking-apps-2">A Note on Apps Invoking Apps</h2>

<p>With iOS 9, Apple has made it easier for an app to directly give control to a different app. A potential danger that lurks on this road is user disorientation. When apps can seamlessly invoke other apps it is easy for users to get confused and think they are in the wrong app. For instance, clicking on a link in Pandora sends users (seamlessly) to iTunes, but since iTunes does not display any logo on the page or in the navigation bar, it’s easy for people to miss that they are in a different app (admittedly slightly less easy now, because the <em>Back-to-Pandora </em>button offers a supplementary cue, should the users be inclined to notice it and make the right inference).</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Pandora Simon and Garfunkel page displaying the Boxer (left); iTunes album page (right) (" border="0" height="558" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/09/23/music.png" width="640"/>

<figcaption><em>Clicking on the shopping-cart icon in Pandora takes users to iTunes. However, because no logo is displayed in iTunes, it’s easy for users to get disoriented and think they are still in Pandora.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Until recently, we used to say that apps don’t need a logo because users usually know what they’ve launched. If you expect your app to be invoked by other apps, <strong>seriously consider adding a logo</strong> in the navigation bar even on deep pages to tell people where they are. Otherwise, they may be not notice that they were switched to a new app and be confused when they don’t see the content that they expect.</p>

<h2 id="toc-conclusion-3">Conclusion</h2>

<p>Being able to directly invoke an app from another app is not a completely new feature in iOS, but the <em>Back-to-app </em>button offers more support to it and allows users to switch easily between two apps. We’ll see if applications will take advantage of this feature and call other apps, or rather, in the spirit of keeping users more and more within their own cage, will prefer to continue serving mobile pages within browsers. In any case, if you’re planning to embrace this feature, make sure that (1) you add information scent to a within-app <em>Back </em>button by labeling it with the name of the page it points to<em>; </em>and (2), you show the app logo on deep pages within your app.</p>
