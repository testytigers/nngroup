<div class="article-content">

<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Email is a powerful way to reach customers, but overdoing it is risky. Let users know up front that you&#x27;ll respect their mailboxes. Otherwise, they won&#x27;t give their email addresses, and you&#x27;ll lose a unique channel for marketing and customer service.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Users are getting very protective of their mailboxes, and you must <strong> minimize the amount of email </strong> you send them. That said, there are two types of email that can enhance customer service and make people buy more:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Acknowledgment email </strong> , sent after a customer makes a purchase. This type of mail alleviates user fears about whether the system <em> really has </em> recorded their order. You can also send a second acknowledgment email when the order has shipped. This is an example of how the online world can be better than the real world: In the latter, it makes no sense to send a letter announcing that a FedEx package is on its way; online, the email will actually reach the customer before the package will and thus can do some good.</li>

<li><strong>Announcements about events that customers <a class="old" href="/articles/request-marketing" title="Alertbox Dec. 2000: Request marketing"> want to be notified </a> about </strong> . For example, a user might ask an airline to send email whenever the price of a ticket to Paris drops below $500. This is another " <strong> better than reality </strong> " feature: In the real world, it's infeasible for travel agents to continually watch for deals that few customers will actually pursue.</li>

</ul>

<p>The key aspect of both examples is that the email is not spam. Rather, the email contains something <strong> of interest to a specific user </strong> that the user either asked for or expected to hear about. Also, both events above benefit from email's "push" nature: They occur at a <strong> specific point in time </strong> , and getting to the customer at that time is necessary if the customer is to benefit.</p>

<p> </p>

<p>Even if a company plans to respect users' time and send only useful and appropriate email, people will still <strong> fear giving out their email </strong> addresses. However, you can entice users to disclose their email address by following these guidelines:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Offer a link to the privacy policy </strong> next to the box where users type in their email addresses.

<ul>

<li>Or, even better: If the policy is ultra-short, just state it right there.</li>

<li>If your legal department wrote the privacy policy, make sure it's edited by somebody who knows how to write for average readers.</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li><strong>Explain how your company will use the address </strong> (for example, only to notify the user of an order's status)</li>

<li><strong>Have an obvious checkbox that lets users opt-in </strong> to receive additional information.

<ul>

<li>Ensure that the checkbox is blank by default, so that users really do opt-in by checking it. Even if you violate this rule and require users to click to opt-out, the very fact that you provide a checkbox increases users' <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce/trust.html" title="Nielsen Norman Group: 34 design guidelines for increasing trust and credibility"> trust </a> that you will respect their preferences.</li>

<li>Explain what the additional mailings will entail and how often you'll send them.</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li><strong>If you offer a newsletter </strong> , link to a sample so that people can assess its quality and relevance.</li>

</ul>

<p>Finally, every time you send users emails that are not direct follow-ups on a user request, always include clear instructions as to how they can unsubscribe.</p>
