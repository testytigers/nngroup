<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>In a miniature information architecture, coverage of a single topic is chunked into units that are connected through simple navigation.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>In our course, <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/information-architecture/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for training tutorial"> IA 1: Structuring and Organizing Web-Based Information</a>, one of the topics is "Mini Information Architecture." This isn't something that's often discussed, and several people have asked me what we mean by this term.</p>

<p>The <strong> definition of mini-IA </strong> is simple: it's how you <strong> structure the information about a single topic</strong>, concept, product, or article. For example, the Alertbox column you're reading now has the simplest possible mini-IA — a single page, with a linear presentation of the information.</p>

<p>When something is covered on a single page, we don't usually think of the presentation as "information architecture." Indeed, the structuring of information within this page would typically be covered in our course <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/writing-2/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for training tutorial"> Writing for the Web 2: Presenting Compelling Content</a>. However, the very <strong> decision to stick to a single-page format </strong> is an IA matter.</p>

<p>Often, it's better to break up information into multiple units rather than use an overly long linear flow. You can then present these multiple units across a few pages or use a within-page navigation system, such as tabs or carrousels.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-linear-paging-usually-bad-1">Linear Paging? Usually Bad</h2>

<p>Let me first dismiss a popular mini-IA as being almost universally bad for usability: If you have a long article, it's almost <strong> never good to simply chop it up into a linear sequence </strong> of pages. If the only navigation is a link saying "continue" or "next page," then it's typically better to stick it all on a single page and <strong> rely on scrolling instead of page-turning</strong>.</p>

<p>(The exception here is for content presented on tablets such as the <a class="old" href="/articles/ipad-usability-year-one/" title="Alertbox: iPad Usability"> iPad</a>, where the swiping gesture provides a <a class="old" href="/articles/generic-commands/" title="Alertbox: Generic Commands"> generic command</a> for moving between pages.)</p>

<p>In many situations, the best alternative is to <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chunking/"> chunk information</a><strong> </strong> into individual content units, focusing on logical cohesiveness; you can then describe each unit accordingly and let users navigate directly to the unit that meets their needs. (Note: "page 3 of 5" is neither descriptive nor deserving of its own page.)</p>

<p>(For wizard-style interactions, such as <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-shopping-carts-checkout-registration/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: E-commerce User Experience -Design Guidelines for Shopping Carts, Checkout, and Registration"> e-commerce checkout</a>, a linear page-turning progression is usually best because even though each step is logically cohesive, they're in an <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/workflow-expectations/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for training tutorial - Application Usability 2: Dialogue and Workflow Design"> application workflow</a>, so you can't go to step 3 without first completing step 2.)</p>

<h2 id="toc-example-usage-relevant-structure-2">Example: Usage-Relevant Structure</h2>

<p>To illustrate a usage-relevant structure, let's look at two example structures that present information about exercises in a mobile fitness app:</p>

<p style="text-align: center; "><img alt="Screenshots of two iPhone fitness apps" height="480" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/mini-ia-examples.png" width="670"/><br/>

Lists of exercises in two iPhone apps:<br/>

<cite>You Are Your Own Gym </cite> (left) and <cite> Full Fitness </cite> (right)</p>

<p>The example on the left employs a useful mini-IA for pushup exercises: it puts all the exercises together in a list that's <strong> ordered from the easiest to the hardest</strong>. In contrast, the example on the right sorts the exercises <a class="old" href="/articles/alphabetical-sorting-must-mostly-die/" title="Alertbox: Alphabetical Sorting Must (Mostly) Die"> alphabetically, which is usually bad</a>.</p>

<p>The <cite> Full Fitness </cite> screenshot (right) shows only a part of the complete list and includes incline pushup, modified pushup, and plain old pushup. How do you know which one to pick if you want a variation that's a bit more challenging than your last exercise? Is "modified" easy or difficult?</p>

<p>"Modified" obviously emits poor <a class="old" href="/articles/information-foraging/" title="Alertbox: Information Foraging - Why Google Makes People Leave Your Site Faster"> information scent</a> — the word tells you what the exercise is <em> not </em> , instead of what it <em> is </em> . "Incline" is better, though not as clear as the equivalent "hands elevated" label used by <cite> You Are Your Own Gym </cite> . Quick, what's the difference between an incline and a decline pushup? I bet you can't answer that as quickly (or as correctly) as you might decipher "hands elevated" vs. "feet elevated." <a class="old" href="/articles/web-writing-use-search-keywords/" title="Alertbox: Use Old Words When Writing for Findability"> Simpler words are usually best</a>.</p>

<p>(Except if you're writing for an expert audience. But advanced fitness enthusiasts definitely won't need to look up how to perform an incline pushup even if that's what they might prefer calling this exercise.)</p>

<p>The designers of <cite> Full Fitness </cite> would have surely benefited from the course on <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/topic/writing-web/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for training tutorial"> writing for online</a>. That said, their main problem is structural. Even with improved labels, the current <cite> Full Fitness </cite> scheme would remain less usable than the <cite> You Are Your Own Gym </cite> solution, which recognizes that pushup variations <strong> deserve their own mini-IA </strong> structured according to the best way to make sense of different pushup exercises. (Here: progressing from easy to hard as you get stronger.)</p>

<p>As an aside, both apps use <strong> thumbnail photos </strong> to further explain the exercises and help users determine which one to choose. And both have usability problems. <cite> You Are Your Own Gym </cite> 's photos have too much background detail to be easily understood given their small size. <cite> Full Fitness </cite> 's photos are cleaner and almost as easy to grasp as the <cite> Own Gym </cite> photos, even though they're much smaller. I usually <a class="old" href="/articles/top-10-web-design-mistakes-of-2003/" title="Alertbox: Top 10 Web Design Mistakes of 2003; see # 4"> criticize tiny thumbnails</a>, but most of the <cite> Full Fitness </cite> images (except for the two machine exercises) are clean enough to adequately differentiate the exercises. (For more on how to select proper images for small screens, see the course on <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/vis-mob-1/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed outline for training tutorial"> Visual Design for Mobile Devices and Tablets</a>.)</p>

<h2 id="toc-usage-driven-structure-3">Usage-Driven Structure</h2>

<p>When you have a lot of information about a topic, there are 3 ways of presenting it:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>One long page </strong> is a simple choice, but makes it harder for users to access individual subtopics. You also risk <strong> intake fatigue </strong> as users slog their way through the page to the bitter end (and <a class="old" href="/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="Alertbox: How Little Do Users Read?"> many will give up</a> before the going gets too bitter).</li>

<li><strong>Mini-IA </strong> lets you split the info into appropriate chunks. This allows direct access to subtopics of interest and can give users a better understanding of the concept space than they'd get while putting their nose to the grindstone to endlessly scroll.</li>

<li><strong>Distributed information </strong> lets you blend together subtopics of many topics, as in the pushup exercises, cable machine exercises, etc. in the <cite> Full Fitness </cite> section on "Chest Exercises."</li>

</ul>

<p>Here, I've argued that usability is often enhanced by the middle approach. However, a mini-IA makes sense only if you can structure this localized information space according to a principle that supports the users' tasks and <a class="old" href="/articles/mental-models/" title="Alertbox"> mental models</a>.</p>

<p>Since the Web's beginning, <strong> internally focused structuring </strong> has been one of the most user-repellent design mistakes. Our research into <a class="old" href="/articles/intranet-information-architecture-ia/" title="Alertbox: Intranet Information Architecture"> intranet IA</a>, for example, has repeatedly found that both usage and employee productivity skyrocket when a department-based IA is replaced by a task-based IA.</p>

<p>Along similar lines, a mini-IA won't help if it's structured according to your internal organizational chart or any other way that fails to match how customers want to access information. But if you embrace a mini-IA — identifying a <strong> usage-based structuring </strong> scheme as a basis for a <strong> clear and modest navigation </strong> system — you'll likely have a winner on your hands.</p>
