<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>If an image works well on a small screen, think about how it will grow and shift to fill the space on a larger monitor. Consider relative screen space and information density, paying close attention to cropping, scaling and proportions.  Show more things, not just bigger things.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Websites are often designed with a mobile-first or mobile-focused approach. But sites that are optimized <strong>for mobile don’t always translate well to larger screens</strong>. Those larger screens may be tablets, laptops, desktops or even 30-inch monitors with 4K resolution. Users of these high-end devices should also be supported by your design.</p>

<p>Previously, we discussed that <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/big-pictures-small-screens/">images designed for large screens often don’t work on small screens</a>. You may have read that article and thought, “My mobile images look great – not to worry.” Now it’s time to see if your desktop images also look great.</p>

<p>Images that work well on small screens may not be appropriate for big ones. They may become too large in relation to surrounding page elements, force extra scrolling, or lose impact. When you’re designing for multiple page sizes, you need to consider orientation (horizontal or vertical), aspect ratio (the proportional relationship between the width and height of the image), size and relationship to surrounding elemetns carefully. </p>

</div><h2 id="toc-common-problem-1-disproportionate-scaling-1">Common Problem #1: Disproportionate Scaling</h2>

<p>In order to resize an image to fit a larger screen, some designs simply expand the height and width of the image to fill the new container. This procedure frequently results in a picture that is <strong>disproportionately large in relation to surrounding page elements</strong> (headlines, text or navigation).  It can cause three main problems: </p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Low <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/utilize-available-screen-space/">information density</a></strong>: The information value of many images is not high enough to justify the space they take up when enlarged for a big screen. This wasted screen space could be used to <strong>show <em>more things</em>, rather than just <em>bigger things</em>.</strong></li>

<li><strong><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/">Scrolling</a></strong>: To get past the image and see some content, users need to scroll. The image becomes an obstacle to get around and increases <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/">interaction cost</a>.</li>

<li><strong>Poor image quality</strong>: Low-resolution images may become pixelated or blurry at larger sizes. In general, any weakness in an image become more apparent, the larger it becomes.</li>

</ul>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="331" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/chipotle_mobiledesktop.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>On Chipotle’s mobile view (left), an image about fresh ingredients appears above a promotion. The same image fills the whole screen on desktop (right). Thus, paradoxically, large-screen users see less information than mobile users. The increase in image size does not increase the image’s value on desktop.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="317" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/lung_mobiledesktop.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>On the American Lung Association’s site, 3 icons and text links from the mobile homepage (left)  are scaled up to occupy an entire desktop screen (right).</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-common-problem-2-cropping-2">Common Problem #2: Cropping</h2>

<p>To keep images from becoming disproportionately large, some designs constrain the height of the image, expanding only the width. This approach effectively crops the image vertically and can keep more items visible on the screen without scrolling, but the resulting image may <strong>lose meaning if it is poorly cropped</strong>.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="303" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/visitseattle_mobiledesktop_2.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>The mobile view of VisitSeattle.com includes a picture of the water, a sailboat and the city skyline. As the page width grows for a larger and wider monitor, the city skyline disappears as well as the top and bottom of the boat.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-common-problem-3-repositioning-3">Common Problem #3: Repositioning</h2>

<p>As images grow to fit larger screens, their relationship to other elements on the screen may change or shift. Pictures may become oddly framed or may overlap with other page elements, <strong>losing meaning or effectiveness</strong>.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="356" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/fatherly_mobiledesktop_2.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>In the mobile view of Fatherly.com (left), the image shows a man in front of a laptop and a small boy next to him. In the desktop view (right), the boy disappears behind the text, the laptop is obscured, and the focus shifts to the man’s head, rather than to the relationship between the two people in the photo. As a result, the photo no longer communicates the point of the article: now it looks like a guy working, rather than a dad needing to focus on his son.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-solution-1-scale-and-crop-4">Solution #1: Scale and Crop </h2>

<p>Pay attention to the details of scaling and cropping an image. In most cases, an image should not simply scale up to fill a larger screen. Restricting only the height can result in a poorly cropped image. Instead, <strong>combine scaling and cropping</strong> to ensure that the resulting image remains appropriately sized, retains its meaning, and fits the screen dimensions appropriately.</p>

<p>For example, as the screen gets wider, scale the image up in width, height, or both, but stop before the image becomes either disproportionately large or hard to understand. Then, crop the image so it works in the new screen dimensions and start expanding this version until the image needs adjusting.  Width, height, and aspect ratio may change as the dimensions of the page change.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="673" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/tinkercrate_together.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>As the screen size increased, the image on Kiwi Crate’s site first grew wider while retaining its height in the design, then shifted to a differently cropped image that expanded horizontally as screen width increased while retaining height. When that image lost meaning due to cropping at the top and bottom, the height of the image was increased slightly to retain the meaning of the image and maximize the space on the larger screen.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-solution-2-use-images-that-work-in-different-aspect-ratios-and-dimensions-5">Solution #2: Use Images that Work in Different Aspect Ratios and Dimensions</h2>

<p>If your site must work across multiple screen sizes, <strong>design with those various dimensions in mind</strong>. Select images that will work well on different devices. Ask the following questions:</p>

<ul>

<li>Can the image look good at a variety of aspect ratios?</li>

<li>Is the image large enough that it could be trimmed from the top, bottom, or sides without impacting the meaning of the picture significantly?</li>

<li>Is there a central feature in the image that you need to keep visible as screen size changes?</li>

<li>Is the image necessary at each screen size: does it add enough value to justify the space it takes in the design?</li>

</ul>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="273" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/blueapron_mobilewide.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>Blue Apron used an image that could work at different sizes and aspect ratios. The mobile image (left) scaled to a maximum height and then the width expanded to expose more of the picture as screen size increased (right).</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="487" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/roomertravel_3b.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption>The Roomer Travel image worked as both a panoramic image on widescreen monitors, with a short and wide aspect ratio (right and indicated in orange in bottom image), and as a vertical image on small screens, with an aspect ratio that was more tall and narrow (left and indicated in red in bottom image).</figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-solution-3-set-a-maximum-dimension-for-the-image-6">Solution #3: Set a Maximum Dimension for the Image</h2>

<p>There may be a dimension beyond which your image won’t work well. In that case, you can <strong>restrict the image size</strong> on the page. Scale and crop until the image loses meaning or quality, and then frame it with white space or content that can fill a wider screen monitor.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="703" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/07/14/marriott_3b.png" width="692"/>

<figcaption><em>Marriott.com increased the size of the image up to a maximum dimension and then added gray borders around the page. (Note the image used in the mobile design is poorly cropped for mobile, showing none of the detail that would allow a viewer to recognize Washington, DC.)</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-solution-4-swap-or-remove-images-7">Solution #4: Swap or Remove Images</h2>

<p>Another option is to <strong>choose different images for different screen sizes</strong>, picking images that work well for specific aspect ratios or ranges of height and width.  In fact, you may find that at some screen sizes, the design is more effective without an image at all.</p>

<p>For example, the image on the Marriott site shown above worked well in a horizontal orientation with a wide aspect ratio, but did not translate well on a mobile site with the image cropped to a square. The site could have used, for instance, an image of the U.S. Capitol dome, or any other representative image that would fit the small, square container.</p>

<p>It’s often too expensive to commission multiple photographs, but if you have a rich set of images to choose from, use different photos if you can find ones that will have optimal impact under different screen size conditions.</p>

<h2 id="toc-dont-only-design-for-the-breakpoints-8">Don't Only Design for the Breakpoints</h2>

<p>Designers and developers can overlook the issues described here if they only design, develop, and subsequently view their designs on a single monitor, or only on devices with their target breakpoints. See what your design looks like at those in-between sizes or on widescreen displays. While you may be optimizing for certain device types, you also need to create a design that is usable and meaningful regardless of device or screen size.</p>
