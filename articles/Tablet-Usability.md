<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Flat design and improperly rescaled design are the main threats to tablet usability, followed by poor gestures and workflow.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>We’ve now conducted 6 rounds of usability studies with tablet users. The good news is that <strong>tablet usability is reasonably solid</strong> and has improved substantially since the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ipad-usability-first-findings/" title="iPad Usability: First Findings From User Testing, article">initial batch of wacky iPad apps</a>, which often totally confused users.</p>

<p>We’ve tested several generations of big and small iPads, as well as many models of Android tablets (including the Kindle Fire) and some Windows tablets (including Microsoft Surface). We found that most <strong>websites are fairly usable on tablets</strong> and need only limited adjustments to suit this environment. (In contrast, using <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-usability-first-findings/" title="Mobile Usability, First Findings, article">websites on mobile phones</a> requires many more <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-site-vs-full-site">design changes to accommodate the smaller screens</a>.)</p>

<p>Not surprisingly, when we asked people how they use their tablets, web browsing was universally mentioned as a top activity.</p>

<p>Although tablet-specific applications have plenty of usability flaws, the problems are mainly the same as those that plague <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/topic/applications/" title="Courses on application usability">traditional application design</a>: difficult features, a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/workflow-expectations/" title="Workflow Expectations: Presenting Steps at the Right Time, article">mismatch with user workflow</a>, and poor instructions that people don’t read.</p>

<p>Designing and building any high-usability application involves substantial work, and tablet apps entail a few additional issues, including the need to modify the user interface for different tablet models. This, combined with the popularity and ease of using websites on tablets, prompts the question of why companies would have a tablet app in the first place. In fact, we advise most companies to <strong>stick to their website</strong> and invest the resources in improving web usability, which still suffers badly in most companies.</p>

<p>Build a tablet app only if you can offer <strong>value-added functionality</strong> over a website, such as creating an app that is focused on supporting a single main task.</p>

<p>In any case, <strong>don’t make your tablet app a scaled-up phone app</strong>. We’ve seen hundreds of apps (mainly on Android) that <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/utilize-available-screen-space/" title="Utilize Available Screen Space, article">misuse screen space</a> by offering tablet users the same basic design as phone users.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-revenge-of-the-frames-1">Revenge of the Frames</h2>

<p>In 1996, I <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-frames-suck-most-of-the-time/" title="Why Frames Suck (Most of the Time), article">condemned the use of frames</a> in web page design. Sure enough, those original terrible frames are rarely seen today; improved design techniques — such as inline frames and parallax scrolling — meet similar goals with better usability.</p>

<p>But, like zombies, certain bad designs come back from the dead to haunt users, and frames-like concepts cause usability problems in many modern tablet designs. Two common problems are split-screen designs and temporary frames for search results and the like.</p>

<p>Although a tablet seems big compared to a phone, it’s still a small screen and typically shouldn’t be subdivided into smaller frames or split views, except when users <em>really</em> need to access two types of information simultaneously. Every time you split off part of the screen, less remains to show content.</p>

<h2 id="toc-web-ux-bleedthrough-2">Web UX Bleedthrough</h2>

<p>Given the web’s dominance in computer use these days, it’s not surprising that we found concepts from the <strong>web user experience bleeding through the platform divide</strong> and influencing people’s use of tablet apps. Key examples here include search dominance and heavy reliance on the <em>Back</em> button.</p>

<p>Users frequently want to <strong>search</strong> on tablets; they also want to return to their search results. Unfortunately, many apps don’t provide a proper SERP (search engine results page) as a primary navigation object that users can easily return to. Instead, search results are shown in one of those zombie-attack frames with a fleeting screen presence.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-1999/" title="The Top 10 Web Design Mistakes of 1999, article"><strong><em>Back</em> button</strong> has long been the user’s lifeline on the web</a>; if anything, it’s even more important on tablets, where accidental activation is a common consequence of the touchscreen interface. Unfortunately, even with apps that did offer <em>Back</em>, our testing revealed periodic usability problems: sometimes the feature was hard to find, while other times it didn’t undo the user’s last action as expected.</p>

<h2 id="toc-gesture-problems-3">Gesture Problems</h2>

<p><a href="http://www.jnd.org/dn.mss/gestural_interfaces_a_step_backwards_in_usability_6.html" title="Gestural Interfaces: A Step Backwards In Usability, article">Gestural user interfaces have several inherent problems</a> that tablet apps need to minimize:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Accidental activation</strong>: users often touch things by mistake and need a way to undo the result.</li>

<li><strong>Swipe ambiguity</strong>: when the screen is divided into subregions (such as the frames we caution against), the same gesture can have different effects, depending on where it’s activated. This problem is exacerbated by the trend toward <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-design/">flat design</a>, which doesn’t clearly demarcate the regions.</li>

<li><strong>Invisibility</strong>: users can’t see the gesture they just made, and they sometimes can’t even see what they’re supposed to touch. Again, flat design makes this worse.</li>

<li><strong>Low learnability</strong>: all of the previous problems combine to make gestures hard to learn. Advanced gestures might as well not exist, as very few users employ anything beyond the basic tap, press, swipe, drag, and pinch gestures.</li>

</ul>

<p>Despite these inherent problems, most of the tablet apps we tested employed gestures reasonably well. The exaggerated skeuomorphism of the early days has also subsided.</p>

<h2 id="toc-dangers-ahead-4">Dangers Ahead</h2>

<p>The two main <strong>threats to tablet usability</strong> are:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Flat design</strong>. Why not allow users to easily see what they can do? We need a golden middle ground between skeuomorphism and a dearth of distinguishing signifiers for UI elements.</li>

<li><strong>Rescaled design</strong>. Whether shoehorned down from a bigger screen or grotesquely exploded from a phone screen, too many Android designs simply don’t fit the tablet’s actual screen size. (Poorly rescaled designs are less common on iPad and Windows tablets, probably because of smaller device diversity.)</li>

</ul>

<p>The flat design threat is a fashionable trend that will hopefully subside before it hurts users (and companies) too much. The second threat will be with us longer, because it’s caused by resource constraints and the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/repurposing-vs-optimized-design/" title="Repurposing vs. Optimized Design, article">naïve idea that a single design is good enough</a> as long as it <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-vs-responsive">adapts to the available screen space</a>.</p>
