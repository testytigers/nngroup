<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>The phrase ‘Learn More’ is increasingly used as a crutch for link labels. But the text has poor information scent and is bad for accessibility. With a little effort, transform this filler copy into descriptive labels that help users confidently predict what the next page will be.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Some trends are subtler than others. Much like <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/low-contrast/">low-contrast text</a>, the use of <em>Learn More </em>as a standalone link label has been quietly trending. The web now has an abundance of links with this generic label, largely tacked on to information of secondary or tertiary importance. (A Google search finds 1.4 billion instances of this term, though some admittedly might be from proper use of the term in general content.) Typically, these links are placed after a short paragraph that briefly introduces a topic, feature, or service, so that the <em>Learn More </em>points the visitor to the detail page. Usually, these links are not the main calls to action on the page, which partly explains why this copywriting detail doesn’t get as much attention or A/B testing as other calls to action.</p>

<p>Most of you have surely seen this pattern. Below is an example of what we’re talking about:</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="'Learn More' link as a secondary call to action" height="494" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2015/11/23/smartthingscom_learnmore.png" style="border: 1px solid darkgray;" width="720"/>

<figcaption><em>Smartthings.com: As a standalone label, </em>Learn More<em> lacks descriptive keywords to help users understand what to expect from the next page. </em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>The proliferation of <em>Learn More </em>links is likely mobile driven: mobile-optimized sites are getting better at <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/defer-secondary-content-for-mobile/">deferring secondary content</a>. That is, instead of making all the details available by default, headings and short paragraphs provide an easy-to-scan, digestible overview of essential content. When users decide they want more information, they can tap a link or expand an accordion to get to the less important content.  This design pattern is definitely beneficial on mobile, and our article is  a critique of the link label only, and not of the general practice of deferring less important content.</p>

<p>We’ll first look at why this phrase is problematic when used by itself, and why it’s worthwhile to be more descriptive. Then we’ll explore how to figure out what to say instead, with examples of sites that have done it well. You’ll even have a chance to try rewriting labels yourself. For that final part, we recommend you have a writing utensil handy, because, yes, there will be a quiz.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-why-learn-more-is-a-problem-1">Why <em>Learn More </em>Is a Problem</h2>

<p>Let us first acknowledge that <em>if </em>the text that precedes the link makes it obvious what’s to come, then a <em>Learn More</em> label isn’t devastating to the user experience. Remember, this article is about <em>how to do better</em>. Descriptive text does help clarify and set expectations. However, even in that situation, there is often ambiguity about the scope of the link. Does the link go to a full page dedicated to the topic? Or does it lead to a landing page where the user will have to choose from a dozen other links? Does it point to an external site or an app store? These are all questions that an informative link label can help answer.</p>

<p>The phrase is most dangerous when used alone because of its ambiguity and <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/">poor information scent</a>. It <strong>creates uncertainty, because users don’t know what to expect</strong> if they click, or whether it will be worthwhile to wait for a new page to load. That feeling of uncertainty can cause users to hesitate and shift into a state of cognitive strain. Some may resort to <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/multi-tab-page-parking/">page parking</a> for evaluating unclear links.</p>

<p>If a webpage has several <em>Learn More </em>links, users may <strong>wonder if the links point to the same page or to different pages</strong>. Once again, uncertainty and cognitive strain have a negative impact on the user experience. Worse, when they do point to the same page, users may waste clicks and be disappointed to discover that the same page is provided. That adds disappointment and confusion to the mix.</p>

<p>Finally, as a standalone link, the lack of descriptive keywords is a <strong>serious problem for accessibility</strong>. People who are visually impaired and using a <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/touchscreen-screen-readers/">screen reader</a> or magnifier aren’t able to quickly glance back to the preceding-paragraph text to see what the <em>Learn More </em>link may refer to.  Most screen-reader users will hear a list of links read to them, with no visual context to help them make sense of different generic <em>Learn</em> <em>More</em> or <em>Read</em> <em>More</em> links.</p>

<p>So, if you use descriptive labels instead of standalone <em>Learn More </em>text, your copy will benefit in several ways:</p>

<ul>

<li>Links will be more accessible.</li>

<li>Links will be more enticing to users and potentially more persuasive.</li>

<li>Users will feel more confident as they click from page to page.</li>

<li>More keywords on the page will help search-engine optimization.</li>

<li><a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/">Meaningful links</a> will stand alone and help users who are <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-web-users-scan-instead-reading/">scanning the page</a>.</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-what-to-say-instead-3-solutions-2">What to Say Instead: 3 Solutions</h2>

<p>Now that you’re on board with rewriting those link labels, let’s see some suggestions for what to write instead. Below are the three most common approaches.</p>

<h3>Option 1: Use keywords that describe the link’s destination.</h3>

<p>This is the most common and, typically, the best approach. To do this, you have to look at the destination page and see what it’s about. Don’t skip that step. When rewriting the labels, remember to try to <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/">front-load the link text</a> by putting the most relevant keywords at the beginning of the text. For example, instead of “Learn How Professional Chefs Cook Squash”, try “Cook Squash Like The Pros.”  Front-loading text makes it much faster for users to scan the page and decide what to do next.</p>

<h3>Option 2. Retain the <em>Learn More </em>format and add descriptive keywords.</h3>

<p>There are times when it is acceptable to retain <em>Learn more</em>, if you qualify it with information about what is to be learned (e.g., <em>Learn</em> <em>more</em> <em>about</em> <em>our</em> <em>services</em>). The downsides to this approach are space constraints for the longer label, and reduced scannability because the relevant keywords appear at the end instead of the beginning of the phrase. Nevertheless, a long link does create a larger target, which is faster for users to click and tap than a small target. In some cases, you might try only linking the descriptive phrase, for example: “Learn more: <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/">How a Link is a Promise</a>.”</p>

<h3>Option 3: Convert the preceding-paragraph heading into the only link.</h3>

<p>If the heading of the preceding paragraph is obviously styled to look like a link and the copy clearly describes what the link points to, then a <em>Learn More </em>link is probably redundant.</p>

<h2 id="toc-conclusion-5">Conclusion</h2>

<p>If you find yourself relying on <em>Learn More </em>or <em>Read More</em> as standalone link labels, remember that they create uncertainty for users and are bad for accessibility. Use the examples and approaches in this article to add descriptive keywords and make your link labels more informative and enticing to users.</p>

<p><strong>Suggestions to Improve Labels</strong></p>

<p>Alternative label suggestions are separated by vertical bars (|).</p>

<ol style="list-style-type:upper-alpha;">

<li>Browse Connected Devices | See What Else You Can Connect</li>

<li>Careers | Careers at BCG | Join Our Team</li>

<li>LSEG Academy Venue Hire | Contact LSEG Academy</li>

<li>What Hue Does | Get the Most Out of Hue at Home</li>

</ol>
