<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Simple usability tests where users think out loud are cheap, robust, flexible, and easy to learn. Thinking aloud should be the first tool in your UX toolbox, even though it entails some risks and doesn&#x27;t solve all problems.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p style="margin-left:40px"><em>"Thinking aloud may be the single most valuable usability engineering method." </em></p>

<p>I wrote this in my 1993 book, <a class="old" href="/books/usability-engineering/" title="Jakob Nielsen: textbook about usability methods"> <cite> Usability Engineering</cite></a>, and I stand by this assessment today. The fact that the same method has remained #1 for 19 years is a good indication of the <strong> longevity of usability methods</strong>.</p>

<p><a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/durability-of-usability-guidelines/" title="Alertbox: Durability of Usability Guidelines">Usability guidelines live for a long time</a>; usability <em> methods </em> live even longer. Human behavior changes much more slowly than the technology we all find so fascinating, and the best approaches to studying this behavior hardly change at all.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-defining-thinking-aloud-testing-1">Defining Thinking Aloud Testing</h2>

<p>To define <em> thinking aloud</em>, I'll paraphrase what I said 19 years ago:</p>

<blockquote>

<p>Definition: In a<strong> thinking aloud test,</strong> you ask test participants to use the system while continuously thinking out loud — that is, simply verbalizing their thoughts as they move through the user interface.</p>

</blockquote>

<p>("Simply" ought to be in quotes, because it's not that simple for most people to keep up a running monologue. The test facilitator typically has to prompt users to keep them talking.)</p>

<p>To run a basic thinking aloud usability study, you need to do only 3 things:</p>

<ol>

<li><a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-recruit-participants-usability-studies/" title="How to Recruit Participants for Usability Studies, Nielsen Norman Group report">Recruit representative users</a>.</li>

<li>Give them representative tasks to perform.</li>

<li>Shut up and let the users do the talking.</li>

</ol>

<h2 id="toc-benefits-of-think-aloud-2">Benefits of Think-Aloud</h2>

<p>The method has a host of advantages. Most important, it serves as a <strong> window on the soul</strong>, letting you discover what users really think about your design. In particular, you hear their misconceptions, which usually turn into actionable redesign recommendations: when users misinterpret design elements, you need to change them. Even better, you usually learn <em> why </em> users guess wrong about some parts of the UI and why they find others easy to use.</p>

<p>The thinking aloud method also offers the benefits of being:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Cheap. </strong> No special equipment is needed; you simply sit next to a user and take notes as he or she talks. It takes about a day to collect data from a <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/" title="Alertbox: Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users"> handful of users</a>, which is all that's needed for the most important insights.</li>

<li><strong>Robust. </strong> Most people are poor facilitators and don't run the study exactly according to the proper methodology. But, unless you blatantly bias users by putting words into their mouths, you'll still get reasonably good findings, even from a poorly run study. In contrast, <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/risks-of-quantitative-studies/" title="Alertbox: Risks of Quantitative Studies"> quantitative (statistical) usability studies are ripe with methodology problems</a> and the smallest mistake can doom a study and make the findings directly misleading. Quant studies are also <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantitative-studies-how-many-users/" title="Alertbox: Quantitative Studies, How Many Users to Test?"> much more expensive</a>.</li>

<li><strong>Flexible. </strong> You can use the method at any stage in the development lifecycle, from early <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/wireframing-and-prototyping/" title="Nielsen Norman Group video: Paper Prototyping, A How-To Training Video"> paper prototypes</a> to fully implemented, running systems. Thinking aloud is particularly suited for <a class="old" href="/articles/agile-user-experience-projects/" title="Alertbox: Agile User Experience Projects"> Agile projects</a>. You can use this method to evaluate any type of user interface with any form of technology (although it's a bit tricky to use thinking aloud with speech interfaces — see report on <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-conduct-usability-studies-accessibility/" title="How to Conduct Usability Studies for Accessibility, Nielsen Norman Group report"> How to Conduct Usability Evaluations for Accessibility</a> for advice on testing with blind or low-vision users who rely on screen readers such as JAWS). Websites, software applications, intranets, consumer products, enterprise software, mobile design: doesn't matter — thinking aloud addresses them all, because we rely on the users doing the thinking.</li>

<li><strong>Convincing. </strong> The most hard-boiled developers, arrogant designers, and tight-fisted executives usually soften up when they get direct exposure to how customers think about their work. Getting the rest of your team (and management) to sit in on a few thinking-aloud sessions doesn't take a lot of their time and is the best way to motivate them to pay attention to usability. (For more on how to motivate teams to deliver superior user experiences, see the <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/ux-basic-training/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: full-day training course"> UX Basic Training</a> course.)</li>

<li><strong>Easy to learn. </strong> We <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/usability-testing/" title="Nielsen Norman Group training course: User Testing">teach the basics in a day</a> and provide thorough <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/in-house-training/" title="Learning by Doing, 2-Day, in-house Usability Testing Course: Nielsen Norman Group workshop">team training in a 2-day "Learning-by-Doing" course</a>. Of course, this doesn't cover all the twists and advanced modifications needed to hang out your shingle as a usability consultant, but the point is that <strong> you don't need these extras </strong> to run basic <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designers-developers-doing-usability/" title="Alertbox: Should Designers and Developers Do Usability?"> tests for your own design team</a>.</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-downsides-of-think-aloud-3">Downsides of Think-Aloud</h2>

<p>Being cheap and robust are huge upsides of qualitative methods such as thinking aloud. But the flip side is that the method <strong> doesn't lend itself to detailed statistics</strong>, unless you run a huge, expensive study. You can certainly do this — I simply don't recommend it for the vast majority of projects. Better to conserve your budget and invest in <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/parallel-and-iterative-design/" title="Alertbox: Parallel &amp; Iterative Design + Competitive Testing = High Usability"> more design iterations</a>.</p>

<p>Other problems:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Unnatural situation. </strong> Unless they're a bit weird, most people don't sit and talk to themselves all day. This makes it hard for test participants to keep up the required monologue. Luckily, users are typically quite willing to try their best, and they quickly become so <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/authentic-behavior-in-user-testing/" title="Alertbox: Authentic Behavior in User Testing"> engaged in the test tasks </a>  that they all but forget that they're in a study. You can show users a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-demo-video/" title="Demonstrate Thinking Aloud by Showing Users a Video">short video demo of a think-aloud session</a> to quickly and vividly explain what's expected of them.</li>

<li><strong>Filtered statements (vs. brain dump). </strong> Users are supposed to say things as soon as they come to mind rather than reflect on their experience and provide an edited commentary after the fact. However, most people want to appear smart, and thus there's a risk that they won't speak until they've thought through the situation in detail. Don't fall for this trap: it's essential to get the user's raw stream of thought. Typically, you have to prompt users to keep them talking.</li>

<li><strong>Biasing user behavior. </strong> Prompts and clarifying questions are usually necessary, but from an untrained facilitator, such interruptions can very easily change user behavior. In such cases, the resulting behavior doesn't represent real use, so you can't base design decisions on the outcome. At the very least, try to identify those cases where you've biased the user so you can discard that small part of the study. (It's worse when you don't know that you've done wrong — then you risk giving the design team bad advice.)</li>

<li><strong>No panacea. </strong> That this one method isn't the only usability tool you'll ever need is not a true downside, as long as you are willing to <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-research-cheat-sheet/" title="UX Research Cheat Sheet">use other methods</a> from time to time. Thinking aloud serves many purposes, but not all purposes. Once you get a few years' experience with usability, you'll want to use a <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/which-ux-research-methods/" title="Alertbox: When to Use Which User Experience Research Methods"> wider range of user research methods</a>.</li>

</ul>

<p>Don't let the downsides get you down. If you haven't tried it before, go run a quick thinking aloud study on your current design project right now. Because these simplified studies are so cheap, <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/weekly-user-testing-tivo/" title="Alertbox: Weekly User Testing: TiVo Did It, You Can, Too"> weekly user testing</a> is completely feasible — so if you make a few mistakes the first time, you can always correct them next week.</p>
