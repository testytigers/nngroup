<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Users hunt for facts online, so factually rich content will attract readers and keep their attention.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Given that users <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="How Little Do Users Read?">spend almost no time visiting the average web page</a>, how do you get people to actually read your website pages?</p>

<p><strong>Emphasize facts.</strong> There's so much <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/blah-blah-text-keep-cut-or-kill/" title="Blah-Blah Text: Keep, Cut, or Kill?">blah-blah on the web</a> that <strong>straight talk stands out</strong>.</p>

<p>Two of our studies offer telling examples of how users search for facts: one study tested with journalists, and the other tested with people using investor relation (IR) pages on corporate websites.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-journalists-the-ultimate-fact-seekers-1">Journalists: The Ultimate Fact Seekers</h2>

<p>When <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/press-area-usability/" title="Press Area and PR Usability">journalists used corporate websites</a> in our tests, they were very interested in finding facts. Sometimes these facts were offbeat, eliciting an <em>“I didn’t know that”</em> response. Sometimes facts were as simple as a CEO’s age.</p>

<p>Journalists typically <strong>scanned past lines of text that seemed too marketing-oriented</strong>. They were always wary (and sometimes cynical) about marketing information:</p>

<blockquote>“We need to characterize the companies, not just say what they say. I look for facts ... . You can smell it if they are trying to cover a bad fact.”</blockquote>

<p>On the Fidelity site, a journalist liked the simple facts about the funds because they provided context for his readers:</p>

<blockquote>“I like this part: ‘We have 290 funds.’ That’s a fact ... . I would print this out. I do like facts.”</blockquote>

<p>A journalist was also impressed with how the Walmart site explained financial information in the annual report:</p>

<blockquote>“This is good because they explain the numbers, and it’s the important numbers that people really want to know. Good that they are up front about it. Here, I can see their sales are over $137 billion, and they’re up from last year. Good to point out they have improved their sales, and almost 20%.”</blockquote>

<p>A journalist visiting the BMW website was impressed by the thoroughness of the safety information:</p>

<blockquote>“Safety record — I would consider that a good piece of info. One of the reasons I think people buy expensive cars is that they will protect them more in an accident or help them prevent an accident. ... This is actually more precise information. This is not a sales pitch. This term, ‘crumple zone,’ I would find use for in my article ... about the lights, these are all high-tech things that I think readers would find interesting. Those are the kinds of specifics I would be looking for.”</blockquote>

<figcaption><em>At a speed of 100 km/h (62 mph), a car drives 28 meters (92 feet) during the time it takes to take your eyes off the road and read the speedometer. This fact is not just a selling point for BMW's head-up display; it's also the kind of compelling content that attracted journalists in our PR study. <h2 id="toc-ir-pages-in-search-of-company-facts-2">IR Pages: In Search of Company Facts</h2>

<p>When <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/investor-relations-ir/" title="Press Area and PR Usability">testing investor relations (IR) pages</a>, we found that investors and financial analysts expect to find pertinent organization information right away. On an IR page, bombarding people with numerous links without offering content is jarring and makes them work too hard to get what they need. It’s fine to have links that lead to more specific information, but provide an overview first.</p>

<p>That overview should include answers to key questions, such as the organization’s:</p>

<ul>

<li>Purpose</li>

<li>Years of operation</li>

<li>Size (including number of employees and locations)</li>

<li>Headquarters location</li>

<li>Annual revenue</li>

</ul>

<p>This information helps users understand your organization’s background, stability, and <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/credibility-and-persuasive-web-design/" title="Nielsen Norman Group full-day training course: Credibility and Persuasive Web Design">credibility</a>. Further, answering these questions succinctly helps you hold users’ interest and establish a proper relationship with them. If this information is hidden or unavailable, your company will appear evasive or unhelpful.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Gaze plot from eyetracking of user reading fact-filled page. Lines show a reading pattern across many of the lines of copy." loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2013/06/09/company-facts-gaze-plot.png" style="width: 400px; height: 498px;"/>

The bold headings and concise paragraphs kept his attention, as the gaze plot shows. </em><em>Blue dots indicate fixations </em><em>(the order and duration of their gazes). </em></figcaption>

<h2 id="toc-just-the-facts-maam-3">"Just the Facts, Ma'am"</h2>

<p>Eyetracking research shows that users' <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/" title="Show Numbers as Numerals When Writing for Online Readers">eyes are attracted by numbers in web content</a>. Why? Because numbers usually represent facts.</p>

<p>Facts are clearly important, but if you include facts or information that is <strong>off-topic</strong>, you’re likely to frustrate your users. Irrelevant news, articles, and references create diversions that sidetrack users from the points you want to make.</p>

<p>In one of our usability studies, a user clicked a link labeled <em>Environment </em>on a company's website and was <strong>offended by irrelevant information</strong>. She commented:</p>

<blockquote>“This has nothing to do with the environment. I want to know how they are protecting trees or saving seals. This is the usual junk: ‘comply with laws’ — well they should; ‘respect human rights’ — doesn’t tell me how; ‘child labor’ — has nothing to do with the environment ... . I am pretty sure this site will not give me any more information. I’ll have to call. Annoying. I am in the Environment category, everything they give me should be about the environment.”</blockquote>

<p>Give people what they want. What a novel way to drive your <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/topic/content-strategy/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: content strategy courses">content strategy</a>. But it works.</p>
