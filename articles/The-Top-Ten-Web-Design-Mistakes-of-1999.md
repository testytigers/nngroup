Summary:&nbsp;

</span>New technology and conventions have led to several new classes of usability problems in Web design.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>The top 10 design mistakes I identified in 1996 are <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/top-ten-mistakes-revisited-three-years-later/" title="Alertbox: Top-Ten Mistakes Revisited Three Years Later"> still bad for web usability</a> and are still found on many websites. So in that sense, not much has changed over the last three years.</p>

<p>But unfortunately <strong>new web technology</strong> and <strong>new applications</strong> for the web have introduced an entirely new class of mistakes. Here are the 10 worst.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-1-breaking-or-slowing-down-the-back-button-1">1. Breaking or Slowing Down the <em>Back</em> Button</h2>

<p>The <em>Back</em> button is the lifeline of the web user and the second-most-used navigation feature (after following hypertext links). Users happily know that they can try anything on the web and <em> always </em> be saved by a click or two on <em>Back</em> to return them to familiar territory.</p>

<p>Except, of course, for those sites that break <em>Back</em> by committing one of these design sins:</p>

<ul>

<li>opening a <strong>new browser window</strong> (see mistake #2)</li>

<li>using an <strong>immediate redirect</strong>: every time the user clicks <em> Back</em>, the browser returns to a page that bounces the user forward to the undesired location</li>

<li><strong>prevents caching</strong> such that the <em> Back </em> navigation requires a fresh trip to the server; all hypertext navigation should be sub-second and this goes double for backtracking</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-2-opening-new-browser-windows-2">2. Opening New Browser Windows</h2>

<p>Opening new browser windows is like a vacuum cleaner sales person who starts a visit by emptying an ashtray on the customer's carpet. Don't pollute my screen with any more windows, thanks (particularly since current operating systems have miserable window management). If I want a new window, I will open it myself!</p>

<p>Designers open new browser windows on the theory that it keeps users on their site. But even disregarding the <strong> user-hostile message implied in taking over the user's machine</strong>, the strategy is self-defeating since it disables the <em> Back </em> button which is the normal way users return to previous sites. Users often don't notice that a new window has opened, especially if they are using a small monitor where the windows are maximized to fill the screen. So a user who tries to return to the origin will be confused by a grayed-out <em> Back </em> button.</p>

<p>(See also special guidelines for <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/open-new-windows-for-pdfs/" title="Alertbox: Open New Windows for PDF and other Non-Web Documents"> opening PDF files in new windows</a>.)</p>

<h2 id="toc-3-non-standard-use-of-gui-widgets-3">3. Non-Standard Use of GUI Widgets</h2>

<p><strong><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/omnichannel-consistency/" title="Alertbox: Consistency in the Omnichannel Experience">Consistency</a> </strong> is one of the most powerful usability principles: when things always behave the same, users don't have to worry about what will happen. Instead, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/do-interface-standards-stifle-design-creativity/" title="Alertbox: Do Interface Standards Stifle Creativity?">they <em> know </em> what will happen based on earlier experience</a>. Every time you release an apple over Sir Isaac Newton, it will drop on his head. That's <em>good</em>.</p>

<p>The more users' expectations prove right, the more they will feel in control of the system and the more they will like it. And the more the system breaks users' expectations, the more they will feel insecure. Oops, maybe if I let go of this apple, it will turn into a tomato and jump a mile into the sky.</p>

<p>Interaction consistency is an additional reason it's wrong to open new browser windows: the standard result of clicking a link is that the destination page replaces the origination page in the <em> same </em> browser window. Anything else is a violation of the users' expectations and makes them feel insecure in their mastery of the web.</p>

<p>Currently, the worst consistency violations on the web are found in the use of GUI widgets such as radio buttons and checkboxes. The appropriate behavior of these design elements is defined in the <a href="https://developer.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/desktop/design" title="Microsoft Windows desktop design guidelines for developers">Windows desktop design guidelines</a>, the Macintosh human interface standard, and the Java UI standard. Which of these standards to follow depends on the platform used by the majority of your users (good bet: Windows), but it hardly matters for the most basic widgets since all the standards have close-to-identical rules. Update: The most useful platform design standards show how components should behave, with examples: <a href="https://standards.usa.gov/components/form-controls/#checkboxes" title="United States official web design standards">U.S. Web Design Standards</a>, and <a href="https://material.io/guidelines/components/selection-controls.html#selection-controls-checkbox" title="Google Material for Android design guidelines">Google's Material design guidelines</a>.</p>

<p>For example, the <a class="old" href="/articles/checkboxes-vs-radio-buttons/" title="Alertbox: Checkboxes vs. Radio Buttons"> rules for radio buttons</a> state that they are used to <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/radio-buttons-default-selection/" title="Radio Buttons: Select One by Default or Leave All Unselected?">select <em> one </em> among a set of options</a> but that the choice of options does not take effect until the user has confirmed the choice by clicking an OK button. Unfortunately, I have seen many websites where radio buttons are used as action buttons that have an immediate result when clicked. Such wanton deviations from accepted interface standards make the web harder to use.</p>

<p>(See also: Non-standard GUI controls were one of the biggest usability problems identified in our usability testing of 46 <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/website-tools-and-applications-flash/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Website Tools and Applications with Flash">web-based applications in Flash</a>. For the latest info, see our full-day seminar on <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/application-ux/" title="Nielsen Norman Group training seminar: detailed course description"> Application Design for Web and Desktop</a>).</p>

<h2 id="toc-4-lack-of-biographies-4">4. Lack of Biographies</h2>

<p>My first web studies in 1994 showed that users want to <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/bylines-for-web-articles/" title="Alertbox: Bylines for Web Articles?">know the people behind information</a> on the web. In particular, biographies and photographs of the authors help make the web a less impersonal place and increase trust. Personality and point-of-view often wins over anonymous bits coming over the wire.</p>

<p>Yet many sites still don't use columnists and avoid bylines on their articles. Even sites with bylines often forget the link to the author's biography and a way for the user to find other articles by the same author.</p>

<p>It is particularly bad when a byline is made into a <code>mailto:</code> link instead of a link to the author's biography. Two reasons:</p>

<ul>

<li>It is much more common for a reader to want to know more <em> about </em> an author (including finding the writer's other articles) than it is for the reader to want to <em> contact </em> the author — sure, contact info is often a good part of the biography, but it should not be the primary or only piece of data about the author.</li>

<li>It breaks the conventions of the web when clicking on blue underlined text spawns an email message instead of activating a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/guidelines-for-visualizing-links/" title="Alertbox: Guidelines for Visualizing Links">hypertext link</a> to a new page; such inconsistency reduces usability by making the web less predictable.</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-5-lack-of-archives-5">5. Lack of Archives</h2>

<p><a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-pages-must-live-forever/" title="Alertbox: Web Pages Must Live Forever">Old information is often <em> good </em> information</a> and can be useful to readers. Even when new information is more valuable than old information, there is almost always <em> some </em> value to the old stuff, and it is very cheap to keep it online. I estimate that having archives may add about 10% to the cost of running a site but increase its usefulness by about 50%.</p>

<p>Archives are also necessary as the only way to eliminate <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/fighting-linkrot" title="Alertbox: Fighting linkrot"> linkrot</a> and thus encourage other sites to link to you.</p>

<h2 id="toc-6-moving-pages-to-new-urls-6">6. Moving Pages to New URLs</h2>

<p>Whenever a page moves, you break any incoming links from other sites. Why hurt the people who send you free customer referrals?</p>

<h2 id="toc-7-headlines-that-make-no-sense-out-of-context-7">7. Headlines That Make No Sense Out of Context</h2>

<p><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/worlds-best-headlines-bbc-news/" title="Alertbox: World's Best Headlines: BBC News">Headlines</a> and other <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/microcontent-how-to-write-headlines-page-titles-and-subject-lines/" title="Alertbox: Microcontent: How to Write Headlines, Page Titles, and Subject Lines"> microcontent</a> must be written very differently for the web than for old media: they are <strong> actionable items </strong> that serve as UI elements and should help users navigate.</p>

<p>Headlines are often removed from the context of the full page and used in tables of content (e.g., <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-homepages-and-category-pages/" title="Nielsen Norman Group Report: Homepages and Category Pages">homepages or category pages</a>) and in search engine results. In either case the writing needs to be very plain and meet two goals:</p>

<ul>

<li>Tell users what's at the other end of the link with no guesswork required.</li>

<li>Protect users from following the link if they would not be interested in the destination page (so no teasers — they may work once or twice to drive up traffic, but in the long run they will make users abandon the site and reduce its credibility).</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-8-jumping-at-the-latest-internet-buzzword-8">8. Jumping at the Latest Internet Buzzword</h2>

<p>The web is awash in money and people who proclaim to have found the way to salvation for all the sites that continue to lose money.</p>

<p><em>Push, community, chat, free email, 3D sitemaps, auctions</em> — you know the drill.</p>

<p>But there is no magic bullet. Most internet buzzwords have some substance and might bring small benefits to those few websites that can use them appropriately. Most of the time, most websites will be hurt by implementing the latest buzzword. The opportunity cost is high from focusing attention on a fad instead of spending the time, money, and management bandwidth on improving basic customer service and usability.</p>

<p>There will be a new buzzword next month. Count on it. But don't jump at it just because Jupiter writes a report about it.</p>

<h2 id="toc-9-slow-server-response-times-9">9. Slow Server Response Times</h2>

<p>Slow response times are the worst offender against web usability: in my <a class="old" href="/articles/who-commits-the-top-ten-mistakes-of-web-design/" title="Alertbox May 1999: Who commits the top-10 Web design mistakes?"> survey of the original "top-10" mistakes</a>, major sites had a truly horrifying 84% violation score with respect to the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/" title="Alertbox: Response Times: The 3 Important Limits">response-time rule</a>.</p>

<p>Bloated graphic design was the original offender in the response-time area. Some sites still have too many graphics or too big graphics; or they use applets where plain or Dynamic HTML would have done the trick. So I am not giving up my crusade to <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-need-for-speed/" title="Alertbox: The Need for Speed"> minimize download times</a>.</p>

<p>The growth in web-based applications, ecommerce, and personalization often means that <strong> each page view must be computed on the fly</strong>. As a result, the experienced delay in loading the page is determined not simply by the download delay (bad as it is) but also by the server performance. Sometimes building a page also involves connections to backend mainframes or database servers, slowing down the process even further.</p>

<p>Users don't care <em> why </em> response times are slow. All they know is that the site doesn't offer good service: slow response times often translate directly into a <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trust-or-bust-communicating-trustworthiness-in-web-design/" title="Alertbox: Trustworthiness in Web Design"> reduced level of trust</a> and they <em> always </em> cause a <strong> loss of traffic as users take their business elsewhere</strong>. So invest in a fast server and get a performance expert to review your system architecture and code quality to optimize response times.</p>

<h2 id="toc-10-anything-that-looks-like-advertising-10">10. Anything That Looks Like Advertising</h2>

<p>Selective attention is very powerful, and web users have learned to stop paying attention to any ads that get in the way of their goal-driven navigation. That's why click-through rates are being cut in half every year and why <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-advertising-doesnt-work-on-the-web/" title="Alertbox: Why advertising doesn't work on the Web">web advertisements don't work</a>.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, users also <a class="old" href="/articles/fancy-formatting-looks-like-an-ad/" title="Alertbox: Fancy Formatting, Fancy Words = Looks Like a Promotion = Ignored"> ignore legitimate design elements that look like</a> prevalent forms of advertising. After all, when you <em> ignore </em> something, you don't study it in detail to find out what it is.</p>

<p>Therefore, it is best to avoid any designs that look like advertisements. The exact implications of this guideline will vary with new forms of ads; currently follow these rules:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong><a class="old" href="/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/" title="Alertbox: Banner Blindness - Old and New Findings">Banner blindness</a> </strong> means that users never fixate their eyes on anything that looks like a banner ad due to shape or position on the page.</li>

<li><strong>Animation avoidance</strong> makes users ignore areas with blinking or flashing text or other aggressive animations.</li>

<li><strong>Pop-up purges</strong> mean that users close pop-up windoids before they have even fully rendered; sometimes with great viciousness (a sort of getting-back-at-GeoCities triumph). I don't want to ban pop-ups completely since they can sometimes be a productive part of an interface, but I advise making sure that there is an alternative way of using the site for users who never see the pop-ups.</li>

</ul>
