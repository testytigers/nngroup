<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Feline users require special considerations, including larger tap target zones for paws, continual animation, and audible vocalization.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>YouTube has recently been dominated by cats. Even the simplest <a href="http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CdEBgZ5Y46U" title="YouTube: Cat playing Fruit Ninja"> video of a cat using an iPad app</a> easily gathers millions of viewers.</p>

<p>Bowing to this takeover, our clients are increasingly asking us, "How can we improve our site or app for cats?" With their lack of opposable thumbs and ever-shifting focus, cats are certainly a challenging target audience.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-user-research-1">User Research</h2>

<p>For our study, we <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-recruit-participants-usability-studies/" title="Nielsen Norman Group FREE report: How to Recruit Participants for Usability Studies"> recruited</a><strong> 16 cats </strong> as test participants: 4 British shorthairs, 3 Persians, 2 Siamese, 2 Maine Coons, 2 Russian Blues, and 3 cats of “unknown origin.” Of the 16, 4 were declawed and 4 were kittens under 3 months old.</p>

<p>All but three of the cats were allowed outside daily, and all of the test users were experienced with traditional cat toys, including balls and milk-jug rings.</p>

<p>We tested a total of <strong> 28 apps </strong> and <strong> 12 websites</strong>, along with apps that were already on the cats' phones or tablets. The study included Android, iOS, and Windows devices.</p>

<h2 id="toc-usability-findings-2">Usability Findings</h2>

<p>The most common usability problem was the <strong> tap target size </strong> on most interfaces. All of the adult cats were clearly frustrated — even hissing — after accidentally closing the apps they were using.</p>

<p>Even though the cats in the study were smaller than most of the human users we’ve tested, the differences between human and feline anatomies necessitate that we modify our <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/mobile-website-and-application-usability/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Mobile Website and Application Usability"> traditional mobile guideline</a> that <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/">tappable areas be at least 1cm</a> in diameter. Given the average cat's paw size, we recommend tap target zones of at least 3 cm (slightly larger than one inch), although a Maine Coon might need more due to their unbelievably enormous mitts.</p>

<p>Reasonable <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/accessibility/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: articles about accessibility for users with disabilities"> accessibility</a> for polydactyl adult cats would require an additional 3 mm.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Photo from usability study showing user reaction to annoying user interface" height="554" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2013/03/30/annoyed-cat-ipad.jpg" width="400"/>

<figcaption>This test user was clearly irritated — even hostile — after accidentally closing Fruit Ninja.</figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Other findings:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Rapid double and triple taps </strong> are common among felines, especially kittens; any response from a multi-tap should be even faster/louder/blinkier than from a single tap.</li>

<li><a href="http://www.asktog.com/columns/022DesignedToGiveFitts.html" title="Bruce 'Tog' Tognazzini: about Fitts' Law for the time needed to acquire a target">Fitts's Law</a> was found to hold for cats. We expect that it will be confirmed for most other mammalian species in future studies.

<ul>

<li>While beyond the scope of the present research, we suspect that Fitts' Law will not hold for slugs and other crawling critters: their time to reach a target is likely to be <em> linear </em> with the distance.</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li><strong>Swiping </strong> is expected to work from any and every direction, so ensure that your targets are extra responsive and include corresponding sounds.</li>

<li><strong>Animation is especially important</strong>, including <strong> blinking</strong>. In fact, if your site or app doesn't animate, it's pretty much useless.

<ul>

<li>This is a revolutionary finding, considering that blinking has been contraindicated in web design ever since it was #3 on the list of <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/original-top-ten-mistakes-in-web-design/" title="Alertbox: Original Top 10 Mistakes in Web Design"> top-10 design mistakes of 1996</a>.</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>A sensory-activated “<strong>pause mode</strong>” is highly suggested, as nearly half the cats randomly stopped what they were doing to lie down on their devices and stretch, nap, or self-groom for extended periods before resuming their tasks.</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-sound-outperforms-the-written-word-3">Sound Outperforms the Written Word</h2>

<p>Other common sources of irritation were the <strong> pointless presence of instructions </strong> as well as any other use of the <strong> English language</strong>. While <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="Alertbox: How Little Do Users Read?"> human users rarely read instructions</a>, the users in this study didn’t read at all.</p>

<p>Fishsticks, a Siamese kitten, didn't bother with the helpful hints for <em> Laser Kitten</em>, opting instead to simply bat the screen repeatedly, expecting "immediate results." On the other hand, the best performing app, <em> Fruit Ninja</em>, jumped right into music, spinning watermelons, and responsive “claw marks.”</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Screenshot of the home screen in the Laser Kitten iPhone app" height="534" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2013/03/30/screenshot-laserkitten-app.png" width="400"/>

<figcaption>Laser Kitten app as tested: Users repeatedly ignored the instructions and went straight to pawing the user interface.</figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-older-cats-lack-focus-4">Older Cats Lack Focus</h2>

<p>One surprising discovery was the complete <strong> lack of attention </strong> from the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/" title="Alertbox: Usability for Senior Citizens"> seniors</a> in the study. It was as if older cats didn't even care. One cat, Stephen Pawking, a 16-year-old British Shorthair, stared at the website for <em> Cat Fancy </em> magazine with total disregard, despite its stunning Flash intro. We thus modified the research protocol and gave Mr. Pawking a mouse-catching app, but — due to a life spent solely indoors — he lacked the necessary predatory skills and failed to react. We were, however, able to gain a rather positive reaction to the <em> Call-A-Cat </em> app, which repeatedly plays the sound of a can opener. This simply underlines the importance of sound.</p>

<p>Overall, but especially for younger cats and <strong> kittens</strong>, the most common feline usage of mobile devices is <strong> game play </strong> — a finding that was also true in <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/" title="Alertbox: Children's Websites: Usability Issues in Designing for Kids"> testing young humans</a>. It’s helpful to note that the younger age group tends to tear up devices due to their hyperactivity and sharper claws. For testing purposes, a strong case and screen protector are advised.</p>

<h2 id="toc-beyond-humancomputer-interaction-5">Beyond Human–Computer Interaction</h2>

<p>When we broaden the user pool beyond the anthropocentric view, it’s no surprise that many of the findings from <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/hci/" title="Nielsen Norman Group full-day training course: Human Computer Interaction (HCI) for Real World Problems"> human–computer interaction (HCI) research</a> must be modified.</p>

<p>Our initial research with feline users has provided a baseline of <strong> FCI guidelines</strong>. As always, we recommend that you conduct your own <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/user-testing/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: articles about user tests and user testing methodology"> user testing</a> if you’re designing for cats. Given this target audience’s reputation for being notoriously independent-minded, they’re less likely than humans to be accepting of bad design.</p>
