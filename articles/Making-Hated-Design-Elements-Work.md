<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Once users reject a design technique due to repeated bad experiences it&#x27;s almost impossible to use it for good because people will avoid it every time.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Users detest many Web design ideas. For some of the worst, see:</p>

<ul>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/most-hated-advertising-techniques/" title="Alertbox">11 most hated advertising techniques </a></li>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-2005/" title="Alertbox">10 Web design mistakes that readers loathe the most </a></li>

</ul>

<p>In our <a class="new" href="/courses/fundamentals/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: full-day training seminar"> Fundamental Guidelines for Web Usability</a> seminar, people often ask me whether these problematic designs are <em> all </em> bad. Couldn't something that often causes usability problems have a <strong> positive use as well</strong>?</p>

<p>Yes, this does happen.</p>

<p><strong>PDF </strong> presents a clear example. Usually, <a class="old" href="/articles/pdf-unfit-for-human-consumption/" title="Alertbox: PDF - Unfit for Human Consumption"> PDF files torment users</a> on websites and intranets. Posting a sales brochure, <a class="old" href="/articles/investor-relations-ir/" title="Alertbox: Investor Relations (IR) Websites"> annual shareholder report</a>, or the <a class="new" href="/reports/intranet-hr-benefits/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Usability guidelines for presenting standard intranet information, including policies, forms, and procedures"> HR manual</a> online as a single, lumpy PDF file is a sure prescription for usability problems. Much better to convert the content into a navigable information space and rewrite it according to the <a class="old" href="/topic/writing-web/" title="Articles about content usability"> guidelines for writing for the Web</a>.</p>

<p>That said, PDF has its uses — they're just not in the realm of interactive information access. When people want to download a report for later reference, PDF shines. Indeed, I use it myself for this purpose. (As an example, see our <a class="new" href="/reports/social-media-user-experience/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Social Media User Experience"> report on social media usability</a>, which is available for free download. And also note how the report link follows the <a class="old" href="/articles/gateway-pages-prevent-pdf-shock/" title="Alertbox: Gateway Pages Prevent PDF Shock"> guideline to direct users to a gateway page</a> where they can download the document, as opposed to linking directly to the PDF file and causing "PDF shock.")</p>

<p>Unfortunately, even <strong> good uses of bad design techniques are usually doomed</strong>. Users hate these designs so much that they <strong> can't overcome their negative first impressions </strong> in the fraction of a second they allocate to stuff they think they don't need.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-splash-screens-1">Splash Screens</h2>

<p>Take splash screens. Yes, they're universally condemned, and we all know that users' only reaction is to go straight for the <em> "skip intro" </em> button. However, a usability study we did with Chinese users earlier this month included a very nice splash screen for the Shanghai Peninsula hotel:</p>

<p style="text-align:center"><img alt="Splash screen from the Peninsula Hotel Shanghai" height="352" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/peninsula-splash-screen.jpg" width="589"/></p>

<p>Technically, this might not qualify as a splash screen because the page does offer some navigation options. Still, in practice, a page with no content except a huge photo serves the same function as a splash screen — and sure enough, the user <strong> immediately clicked away</strong>.</p>

<p>This user reaction is exactly what I'd typically expect; it's not news that people detest splash screens.</p>

<p>In this case, however, the user was planning a business trip to Shanghai and was particularly interested in learning the hotel's location: was it convenient for her meetings? The splash screen <strong> answers this question in a single look</strong>: You can see immediately that the hotel is right on the Bund. (The most prestigious address in Shanghai, though on the opposite side of the Huangpu River from the Pudong business district.)</p>

<p>Unfortunately, the test participant didn't allocate any attention to the splash screen even though it could have offered her high usability by addressing one of her top concerns.</p>

<p>Bad experiences with many previous splash screens made the user dismiss this one. Instead, she went immediately for the navigation menus, trying to find a "location" link. (See separate guidelines for <a class="old" href="/articles/store-finders-and-locators/" title="Alertbox: Store Finders and Locators"> location finders</a>.)</p>

<p>It's almost impossible for any single website to overcome the cumulative effect of users' visits to countless other sites. (Remember <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/videos/jakobs-law-internet-ux/" title="2 min. video w. Jakob Nielsen">Jakob's Law of the Internet user experience</a>: users spend most of their time on <em> other </em> sites.)</p>

<p>Would I advise the Peninsula to remove the photo? No, I actually think it's a clever illustration. But ironically, it would have more impact if it were smaller and combined with a bulleted list highlighting hotel features — including a bullet for something like "right on the Bund (see photo)."</p>

<h2 id="toc-lightbox-overlay-dialog-boxes-2">Lightbox Overlay Dialog Boxes</h2>

<p>A few years ago, I named <a class="old" href="/articles/10-best-application-uis/" title="Alertbox: Application Design Showcase - 10 Best App UIs"> lightboxes the interaction design technique of the year</a>. In a test we did in Australia last week, a lightbox helped a user looking for flights on the Webjet site:</p>

<p style="text-align:center"><img alt="Iterative user interface design: conceptual process model" height="475" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/webjet-good-lightbox.jpg" width="593"/></p>

<p>Note the design's usability features:</p>

<ul>

<li>It's <strong> contextual </strong> : shown only to Australian users.</li>

<li>It's <strong> simple</strong>: only two choices and one button, and the most likely choice is preselected as the <a class="old" href="/articles/the-power-of-defaults/" title="Alertbox: The Power of Defaults"> default value</a>.</li>

<li>It clearly highlights the <strong> customer benefits </strong> of going to the Australian site, even though the user typed in the URL for the American site.</li>

</ul>

<p>In last week's study, this design worked well, just as many other lightbox designs have performed swimmingly in our previous usability studies.</p>

<p>Sadly, later in the week, we came across this repugnant perversion of an overlay dialog box on another Australian site:</p>

<p style="text-align:center"><img alt="Iterative user interface design: conceptual process model" height="483" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/sharestobuy-bad-lightbox.jpg" width="571"/></p>

<p>Although implemented differently, this is <strong> no better than a pop-up</strong>, which is the <a class="old" href="/articles/most-hated-advertising-techniques/" title="Alertbox"> #1 most hated advertising design</a>. Just as we saw with the Chinese splash screen, our Australian test user <strong> aggressively dismissed </strong> the pop-up-like overlay. One of the lessons of these two weeks of testing is that people are the same all around the world: when they despise a Web design they immediately get rid of it.</p>

<p>(See separate guidelines for usable <a class="old" href="/articles/e-mail-newsletters-increasing-usability/" title="Alertbox: E-Mail Newsletters - Increasing Usability"> subscription interfaces for email newsletters</a>.)</p>

<p>Obnoxious abuse of a Web design element will ultimately <strong> poison the well </strong> for decent websites as users start shunning that design element, even when it's well intentioned.</p>

<p>Annoying blinking banner ads were probably the first design approach to go south and destroy a website's ability to include any design elements of a similar shape. In 1999, I declared <em> "anything that looks like advertising" </em> to be #10 on my <a class="old" href="/articles/the-top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-1999/" title="Alertbox: The Top 10 Web Design Mistakes of 1999"> list of top Web design mistakes</a>. Every year since, studies have shown ever-stronger <a class="old" href="/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/" title="Alertbox: Banner Blindness - Old and New Findings"> banner blindness</a>.</p>

<p>For now, the lightbox overlay remains a useful design technique. Please don't destroy it for all of us by abusing it.</p>
