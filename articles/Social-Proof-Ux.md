<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>People are guided by other people’s behavior, so we can represent the actions, beliefs, and advice of the crowd in a design to influence users.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>In his book <em>Influence: Science and Practice</em>, Robert Cialdini identifies six principles of influence:</p>

<ul>

<li><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/reciprocity-principle/">Reciprocation</a></li>

<li>Social proof</li>

<li><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/liking-principle-ui-design/">Liking</a></li>

<li><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scarcity-principle-ux/">Scarcity</a></li>

<li><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/authority-principle/">Authority</a></li>

<li><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/commitment-consistency-ux/">Commitment &amp; consistency</a></li>

</ul>

<p>In this article, we discuss the second principle in this list: social proof.</p>

<p><strong>Social proof</strong> is a psychological phenomenon where people <strong>reference the behavior of others</strong> to guide their own behavior. This tendency is driven by our natural desire to behave “correctly” under most circumstances—whether making a purchase, deciding where to dine, determining where we should go, what we say, who we say it to, and so on. One of the best examples of social proof, in real life, is the long line in front of an Apple Store on the day a new iPhone is released. The fact that a group of people find the new phone so desirable as to invest considerable time standing (or sleeping!) in line impacts our perception of the phone value (and makes us covet one, too).</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="people in line at Apple Store" height="498" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/09/12/apple-store-queue.jpg" width="700"/>

<p>If all these other people want it, it must be good. That’s why McDonald’s put up a sign saying “over 100 million hamburgers sold” in 1958.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-examples-of-social-proof-interface-patterns-1">Examples of Social-Proof Interface Patterns</h2>

<p>Web 2.0 has popularized the practice of making users’ actions and opinions public with the intent of influencing and informing others. Here are just a few examples where the social-proof principle is at work.</p>

<h3>Reviews</h3>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="customer reviews" height="529" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/09/12/social-proof-adagio-teas.png" width="700"/>

<figcaption><em><a href="http://www.adagio.com/">Adagio Teas</a> uses reviews to influence prospective buyers: “If others like this tea, then maybe I’ll like it as well.”</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>Behavior (Social filters)</h3>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="social filters" height="534" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/09/12/social-proof_ebags.png" width="700"/>

<figcaption><em><a href="http://www.ebags.com/">eBags</a> displays related bags based on what other customers (who viewed the same page) also viewed; “If they viewed this bag, the others they looked at must also be something I would like.”</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3><strong>Opinion</strong></h3>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="people who like an item" height="446" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/09/11/etsy-admirers.png" width="700"/>

<figcaption><em>In this example from Etsy, the interface includes a number and usernames of people who “admire” a particular item. Stores on Etsy often include unique or limited quantity of items, so the presentation of other interested parties is also tapping into the effects of the <a href="/articles/scarcity-principle-ux/">Scarcity principle</a>.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-background-of-the-social-proof-principle-2">Background of the Social-Proof Principle</h2>

<p>Social proof is one of the 6 influencing principles detailed in Dr. Robert Cialdini’s book <em><a href="http://www.amazon.com/dp/006124189X?tag=useitcomusablein">Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion</a></em> (first published in 1984). As a psychology professor, Cialdini and his students conducted numerous research studies to identify and prove these principles. Social proof plays off our insecurities and desire to do the “right thing.”</p>

<p>Occasionally, in usability studies, users tell us that they don’t care about user reviews, they don’t trust other people’s opinions, and they make all of their decisions based totally on their own independent perspective. Unfortunately, thousands of psychology studies prove this “lone wolf” theory to be quite false — another example of why we should <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-rule-of-usability-dont-listen-to-users/">base design decisions on what users <em>do</em> rather than what they <em>say</em></a>.</p>

<p>Social-psychology studies have repeatedly indicated our conscious and unconscious reliance on each other for cues in almost all decisions that we make. One of the most ubiquitous uses of social proof is the universally disliked “laugh track” used in sitcoms. Cialdini notes, “Experiments have found that the use of canned laughter causes an audience to laugh longer and more often when humorous material is presented and to rate the material as funnier. In addition, some evidence indicates that canned laughter is most effective for poor jokes.”</p>

<h2 id="toc-when-to-use-the-technique-3">When to use the technique</h2>

<p><strong>Increasing credibility: </strong>Users do consider how others perceive content, services, and products that they find online. Adding an indication that other people, or even better, familiar people, like the content or product can remove decision-making uncertainty.</p>

<p><strong>Encouraging adoption and acceptance:</strong> If you’re trying to get people to subscribe to a newsletter or follow a Twitter account, communicating that you already have a large number of subscribers can increase subscriptions because it indicates that others like your content. (Example: the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/subscribe/">subscription page for our own newsletter</a>. Please indulge us in using our own site as a good example just this once :-)</p>

<h2 id="toc-testing-social-proof-4">Testing Social Proof</h2>

<p>The most significant risk with using social proof is the perception that too few people approve of the piece of content, service, or product. In a recent usability session, a user looked at the number of people who had shared an article and stated that the article wasn’t popular enough, so maybe it wasn’t good.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="161" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/09/12/atlantic-sharing-buttons-bottom.png" width="452"/>

<figcaption><em>When a study participant noticed that only 1000 people had shared the article on Facebook, she wasn’t impressed. Of course, the 17 G+ users are even less impressive.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>The other risk of social-proof elements is making the interface too busy and (potentially) the page load too slow. Often social widgets result in complex backend communication with social-network sites (such as Facebook and Twitter) and <a href="/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/">significantly increase the response time</a> of the site, especially on mobile devices or in locations with poor connectivity.</p>

<h2 id="toc-conclusion-5">Conclusion</h2>

<p>The use of social proof in interfaces has become commonplace in many web environments. More sophisticated implementations not only indicate general adoption or preference, but highlight adoptees that are known persons (e.g., Facebook friends). Because of the success of social proof, some sites may over-use the technique and complicate simple interactions. This is why it is important to test the specific design implementations and not simply accept that <em>any</em> social proof feature is beneficial.</p>
