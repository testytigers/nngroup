</div><h2 id="toc-break-rule-4-avoid-using-the-word-very-1">Break Rule #4: Avoid Using the Word <em> Very </em></h2>

<p>Third-grade English teachers gave the word <strong> <em> very </em> </strong> a very bad name with their assignments to write essays that include a particular number of words. Some pupils abused <em> very </em> in order to achieve the required word count in their homework. For example:</p>

<blockquote><em>My summer vacation was very good. My very close family made the very long drive across the very beautiful and very, very varied United States. My father did most of the driving, very fast, which made us very, very anxious. But, overall it was a very, very, very good trip and summer vacation. </em></blockquote>

<p>It’s unfortunate that today’s professional communicators avoid <em> very </em> because of this juvenile misuse of the word. <em> Very </em> is a helpful adjective and adverb, and it can quickly and easily magnify the meaning of so many verbs and nouns. We should use <em> very </em> very much more on our websites today.</p>

<p>Let’s consider an example on the Green Mountain Coffee website. The “Behind the Bean” passage uses no <em> very’s: </em></p>

<p><strong>Original content: </strong> Coffee is more than just something to drink. It connects us to each other, and to the rest of the world. We’re passionate about great coffee and we’re anxious to share that passion with you. So join us in this glance at the surprisingly rich and complex story of coffee — how and where it’s grown, the many ways it can be prepared, and the artistry involved along the way.</p>

<p><strong>Our rewrite adding <em> very: </em> </strong> <em> </em> Coffee is <strong> very </strong> much more than just something to drink. It connects us to each other, and to the rest of the world. We’re <strong> very </strong> passionate about <strong> very </strong> great coffee and we’re <strong> very </strong> anxious to share that passion with you. So join us in this glance at the <strong> very </strong> surprisingly rich and <strong> very </strong> complex story of coffee — how and where it’s grown, the <strong> very </strong> many ways it can be prepared, and the artistry involved along the way.</p>

<p>Notice how in our rewrite the injection of <em> very </em> achieves the following 2 goals that the plain text did not:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>demonstrates genuine emotion </strong> by adding <em> very </em> before <em> passionate </em> and <em> anxious </em></li>

<li><strong>reinforces </strong> just how multifaceted the story of coffee is by adding <em> very </em> before <em> complex </em> story of coffee</li>

</ul>

<p>In another example, consider Pottery Barn’s product description for their “Scroll Tile Rug,” and how it could benefit from peppering <em> very </em> throughout.</p>

<p><strong>Original content: </strong> Moroccan tile patterns were the inspiration for this hand-tufted wool rug. The allover scroll motif is tufted in creamy ivory over a solid ground for a versatile look that goes well with a variety of furnishings and decor.</p>

<ul>

<li>Hand tufted of pure wool.</li>

<li>Yarn dyed for vibrant, lasting color.</li>

</ul>

<p><strong>Our rewrite adding <em> very</em>: </strong> Moroccan tile patterns were the inspiration for this <strong> very </strong> hand-tufted wool rug. The allover scroll motif is <strong> very </strong> tufted in <strong> very </strong> creamy ivory over a <strong> very </strong> solid ground for a <strong> very </strong> versatile look that goes <strong> very </strong> well with a variety of furnishings and decor.</p>

<ul>

<li>Hand tufted of <strong> very </strong> pure wool.</li>

<li>Yarn dyed for <strong> very </strong> vibrant, <strong> very </strong> lasting color.</li>

</ul>

<p>In the above case, the use of <em> very </em> has very positive impact because it answers the user’s likely questions before he even has the chance to ask them:</p>

<ul>

<li>Just how “hand-tufted” is the wool rug?</li>

<li>What level of creamy is the ivory?</li>

<li>How solid is the ground?</li>

<li>How versatile is the look?</li>

<li>How vibrant is the color?</li>

<li>How lasting is the color?</li>

</ul>

<p>The expressive answer to each of these questions is simple, descriptive, and the same: <em> very. </em></p>

<p>Our <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/e-commerce-usability/"> recent research on e-commerce user experience</a> found that big product photos are very helpful to users. Big words are also very appreciated.</p>

<p>As very adaptable and very eloquent <em> very </em> can be, do be somewhat disciplined in your use of the double <em> very</em>, as in, <em> Very, very high HD quality. </em> While the <em> very, very </em> can have unusually high impact, it can sometimes seem desperate to readers if overused.</p>

<p>Be especially careful using the triple <em> very</em>, as in, <em> The New England winter of 2014 was very, very, very cold. </em> Too many <em> very's </em> in a row can seem disingenuous and can even impact your site’s <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/credibility-persuasion/"> credibility</a>. If you choose to employ the triple very, use it in no more than 2 instances on your site. And, consider building up to it by using the single <em> very </em> first, then the double <em> very</em>, and finally the triple <em> very</em>. For example, we could better write a passage on our own website by using multiple <em> very’s: </em></p>

<p><em>Since 1998 Nielsen Norman Group has been a <strong> very </strong> leading voice in the user experience field: conducting <strong> very, very, very </strong> <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-usability-cats/"> groundbreaking research</a>, evaluating interfaces of all shapes and sizes, and guiding <strong> very, very </strong> critical design decisions to improve the bottom line. </em></p>

<p>The above example does pose a minor breach of the recommendation by introducing <em> very, very, very </em> before <em> very, very</em>; but you can see the powerful impact and why it was worth this deviation in this particular case.</p>

<p>A final guideline: while one very is good, 2 verys are very good, and 3 verys are very, very great, we recommend not using the sequence <em> very, very, very, very, </em> because our usability studies have shown that some users have difficulty <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/short-term-memory-and-web-usability/"> keeping that many items in short-term memory</a> while retaining some remaining <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/">cognitive capacity</a> to also attend to your main message.</p>

<h2 id="toc-break-rule-5-use-exclamation-points-judiciously-2">Break Rule #5: Use Exclamation Points Judiciously</h2>

<p>Wordsmiths have baptized the exclamation point (!) a cheap attempt at forcing excitement upon readers. In the absence of actual exciting prose, writers tack on an exclamation point at the end of a sentence, as if to tell the reader “this is exciting,” or “be interested in this.”</p>

<p>Back in 2001 we had "avoid exclamation marks" as guideline #26 out of the <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/113-design-guidelines-homepage-usability/"> 117 design guidelines for homepage usability</a>. Normally it is true that human nature doesn't change much from one decade to the next and <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-guidelines-change/"> user experience guidelines therefore also don't change much over the years</a>. However, exclamation points are clearly an exception to this finding! As one of our recent test users said about a site that had been updated to modern design through both <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/infinite-scrolling/"> infinite scrolling</a> and extensive exclamation points: <em> "You can really tell how much these dudes love their site! So much content! So many exclamation points!!" </em></p>

<p>The exclamation point is the most thrilling punctuation available! With this in mind, the recommendation today is to punch up your site’s content and use them copiously. Some of the reasons and user behaviors behind this recommendation include the following:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Users scan frequently and miss subtle enthusiasm in content. </strong> When scanning, people may fail to grasp the fervor embedded in the content itself. An exclamation point can tell users that the content is, indeed, very, very stimulating!</li>

<li><strong>Eyetracking reveals that users are attracted to exclamation points. </strong> Our <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/eyetracking/"> eyetracking research</a> shows that one exclamation point may not attract much attention, mainly because alone it resembles the letter “I” or the number "1." But two exclamation points do attract attention!! And three exclamation points attract even more attention!!!</li>

<li><strong>Gives the period a break! </strong> Readers get bored and sometimes even fatigued reading many sentences in a row that all end with periods. Too many periods can make the web writers and designers seem lazy and unimaginative!!! Injecting exclamation points, double exclamation points, and triple exclamation points shows that you earnestly attempted to provide the most helpful, interesting content!</li>

</ul>

<p>As with the previous usability guideline recommending against the use of 4 <em> very </em> s, it's also best to avoid !!!! Three exclamation points give the best combination of high impact and <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/"> low cognitive load!!!</a> (The guideline against using 5 or 6 exclamation points is even stronger, due to findings from perceptual psychology discussed further in the course on <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/human-mind/"> The Human Mind and Usability</a>: the time taken to count the number of exclamation points to discern the clearly crucial distinction between !!!!! and !!!!!! sadly adds too much <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/"> interaction cost</a> to an otherwise subtle design element.)</p>

<h2 id="toc-break-rule-6-avoid-using-chat-abbreviations-in-website-content-3">Break Rule #6: Avoid Using Chat Abbreviations in Website Content</h2>

<p>Traditionally, web writers recommend using abbreviations judiciously and avoiding jargon. But today, more adults and even <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens/"> seniors</a> are “talking text.” IOW, they understand text abbreviations commonly used in texts, chats, and emails!</p>

<p>Recalling the meaning of “texteviations” (not to be confused with textonyms) is another case of <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/magnifying-glass-icon/"> mobile usage influencing desktop users</a>’ expectations and behavior.</p>

<p>ITA that some abbreviations can be confusing IRL. OTOH, they have their benefits b/c they:</p>

<ul>

<li>decrease word count on the site</li>

<li>intensify a youthful <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/">tone of voice</a></li>

<li>are familiar to users, and thus increase their comfort level on the site and draw on knowledge that the users already have</li>

</ul>

<p>Best of all: fewer words + <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-use-search-keywords/"> shorter words</a> = lower scores in a readability formula, making it laughably easy to comply with the rule to <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/writing-for-lower-literacy-users/"> write at an 8th grade reading level</a> to reach a broad consumer audience.</p>

<p>Grammar geeks may balk. AAR8 texteviations in your site’s content can make for a better user experience than using the spelled out words, particularly since we know that <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/"> numerals enhance scannability</a>.</p>
