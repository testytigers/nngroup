<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Intranets are improving findability and discoverability by organizing content by task rather than department, using mega-menus to present deep content, offering clear cues to help orient users, and providing shortcuts to important pages and tools.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Intranets contain a tremendous amount of information that needs to be easily accessible. Implementing the right information architecture (IA) and using appropriate navigation elements are essential to a successful intranet and a good user experience.</p>

<p>On top of that, intranet IA designers face a tremendous challenge: they have limited sources of inspiration. Because of the very nature of intranets, designers often have few opportunities to see how other intranets structure their information.</p>

<p>To provide intranet designers with a set of ideas and solutions, we analyzed <strong>77 intranet information architectures</strong> and presented their detailed navigation structures, screenshots, and our analysis in the 2nd edition of our <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/intranet-information-architecture-design-methods/" title="Intranet Information Architecture Design Methods and Case Studies, 2nd Edition, Nielsen Norman Group Report">Intranet IA Design Methods and Case Studies report</a>.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/intranet-ia-methods/" title="Intranet Information Architecture (IA) Methods article">first edition was published in 2007</a>, so comparing the two sets of case studies allows us to identify new trends in intranet IA. The biggest change lies in the <strong>navigational design elements</strong> used, not in the topics presented in the main menu. In this 7-year period, changes at the page level have completely outpaced changes at the IA level. <strong>Page-level intranet design has been transformed</strong>, as documented in the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/intranet-design-annual/">Intranet Design Annual</a> series.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-confusion-between-categories-1">Confusion Between Categories</h2>

<p>Many intranets suffer from confusing menu labels. Such labels make it difficult for users to find what they’re looking for.</p>

<p>The most common reasons for confusion are:</p>

<ul>

<li>Terms are <strong>too broad </strong>and end up acting as catch-all (example: <em>For Employees</em>).</li>

<li>Sections such as <em>How do I …</em> or <em>Tools</em> grow <strong>too big</strong>.</li>

<li>Different section names on the site <strong>lack distinction</strong> (example: <em> Human Resources</em> and <em>Administration &amp; Management</em> may appear in the same menu).</li>

<li>Language is polluted with <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/category-names-suck/" title="Avoid Category Names That Suck"><strong>jargon </strong>or branded terms</a>.</li>

</ul>

<p>Unclear naming is one of the biggest and most important projects to tackle when it comes to the intranet IA. All navigation categories must be descriptive, specific, and mutually exclusive, so that users can navigate without hesitation.</p>

<p>User-research methods such as <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/" title="Card Sorting: Pushing Users Beyond Terminology Matches">card sorting</a>, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tree-testing/" title="Tree Testing: Fast, Iterative Evaluation of Menu Labels and Categories">tree testing</a>, and <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-conduct-usability-studies/" title="How to Conduct Usability Studies, Nielsen Norman Group Report">usability testing</a> can help identify ambiguous labels and unclear information groupings.</p>

<h2 id="toc-organizing-the-intranet-by-task-or-topic-2">Organizing the Intranet by Task or Topic</h2>

<p>Task-based structures often better withstood <strong>organizational change </strong>than intranets organized by department. In many cases, intranets are initially structured to mimic the organizational configuration of the company, because this type of IA makes maintenance easy: each department or area gets a section of the intranet. The downside, however, is that whenever the institution reorganizes, the navigation must also change. We have also found in our user testing of intranets that task-based navigation tends to facilitate learning.</p>

<p>Luckily, most intranets we analyzed adopted at least a partial task-based organization scheme. By 2014, <strong>86% of the new intranet IAs were task based</strong>, or at least mostly organized by task or topic. <em>Task-based</em> means that the content organization was independent of who owned or created the content.</p>

<p>A common pitfall with task-based IAs is difficult-to-scan category names. Category names do not need to start with verbs or follow an “I need to ...” pattern in order to be task based. Sometimes trying to fit link or menu labels into a specified format makes them long and more difficult to scan, because the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/" title="First 2 Words: A Signal for the Scanning Eye">most meaningful words don't appear until the end of the label</a>. Task-based IA doesn't require any particular grammatical structure for labels; it just means <strong>grouping information according to how employees use it, rather than by who creates and maintains it</strong>.</p>

<h2 id="toc-number-of-main-navigation-items-3">Number of Main Navigation Items</h2>

<p>Across the 77 intranet IAs in this report, there are an <strong>average number of 7.6 top-level categories</strong>, with a low of 3 and a high of 31 categories. The median number of categories was 7. These numbers did not change much between the editions of this report, published 7 years apart.</p>

<p>What did change between editions was the maximum number of navigation categories. In 2007, we saw more organizations with very broad menus. 11% of companies then had more than 12 categories, while none did in 2014.</p>

<p>This shift toward fewer total categories is good. A broad structure shows a wide range of the available content, but <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-vs-deep-hierarchy/" title="Flat vs. Deep Website Hierarchies">too many categories can make it difficult</a> for users to choose a site area, because of potential overlap in the meaning of the many menu items.</p>

<h2 id="toc-topics-and-labels-in-top-level-navigation-4">Topics and Labels in Top-Level Navigation</h2>

<p>Many intranets followed general patterns for picking main-menu items. 3 topics attained top-level navigation status on the majority of the intranets:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>company </strong>information (66%)</li>

<li><strong>human-resources </strong>information (64%)</li>

<li><strong>news </strong>(56%)</li>

</ul>

<p>Information about <strong>departments </strong>or divisions was a top-level category in 45% of intranets, and <strong>support services</strong> (common needs across organization) appeared at the top in 40% of intranets. There was a very long tail of additional categories found in a smaller proportion of intranets.</p>

<p>Besides these most common topics, companies in the same industry tended to include the same categories at the top of their IA. For example, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/best-industrial-product-manufacturing-intranets" title="Best Industrial, Product, and Manufacturing Sector Intranets, Nielsen Norman Group Report">manufacturing companies</a> often included a product-related category in their top-level navigation, whereas <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/best-knowledge-intensive-business-intranets" title="Best Knowledge-Intensive Business Intranets, Nielsen Norman Group Report">companies with a focus on intellectual property</a> often presented a top-level, knowledge-management (KM) category.</p>

<h2 id="toc-improved-wayfinding-5">Improved Wayfinding</h2>

<p>One noticeable improvement in design between the two editions of this report was in how intranets help users orient themselves within the site. Almost all the intranets in the 2nd edition provided a consistent navigation experience across site areas. The use of templates and design guidelines helped many organizations create a similar look and feel across different pages, even with many content contributors.</p>

<p>Additional <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/navigation-you-are-here/" title="Navigation: You Are Here">wayfinding cues</a>, such as <strong>highlighting the users’ current location</strong> in the site and presenting <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/breadcrumb-navigation-useful/" title="Breadcrumb Navigation Increasingly Useful">breadcrumbs</a>, are now standard on many intranets. These cues are especially important when users are <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/deep-linking-is-good-linking/" title="Deep Linking is Good Linking">deep-linked</a> into the intranet from, for example, a link in an email or a search result.</p>

<p><strong>Clear and persistent navigation</strong> shows users how to find information and helps them understand which site areas they’ve already explored.</p>

<h2 id="toc-mega-menus-6">Mega-menus</h2>

<p>Of the 21 organizations added for the 2nd edition of this report, 9 implemented mega-menus from the main navigation. <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mega-menus-work-well/" title="Mega Menus Work Well for Site Navigation">Mega-menus are large drop-down menus </a>usually divided into groups of navigation options. Mega-menus frequently present both secondary and tertiary pages, thereby making lower-level content more visible to users. This exposure of lower-level topics can drastically <strong>improve discoverability</strong> of previously difficult-to-find information or applications (but it's not a solution for users on mobile devices).</p>

<p>What was presented in the mega-menus varied by organization. Although most chose to list all links, some highlighted only the most important pages to avoid overwhelming users.</p>

<p>Sage and Architect of the Capitol didn’t just list links but also included a featured area to promote specific content from that site section.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Sage 3 column megamenu" height="424" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/02/19/Sage-Megamenu%20w%20callouts.png" width="720"/>

<figcaption><em>Sage divided their mega-menus into two areas:<br/>

1. The right side of the mega-menu lists the most popular pages in that site section. This list allows people to jump into that section without overwhelming them with all the possible pages.<br/>

2. The left side of the mega-menu is used to promote content within that site section. </em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Top image with 1 column dropdown menu, bottom image with 3 column dropdown menu" height="577" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/02/19/PCL_simple%20and%20megamenu.png" width="720"/>

<figcaption><em>PCL Constructors, Inc. implemented a mega-menu only for the navigation items that needed it. </em>Projects<em> has only four secondary links and can do with a simple drop-down, whereas the </em>Districts<em> section contains many more links. Presenting the </em>Districts<em> menu as a drop-down would require users to scroll to see the menu and would tax their fine motor-skills and short-term memory.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-customizable-quicklinks-and-social-filters-7">Customizable <em>Quicklinks</em> and Social Filters</h2>

<p>Many intranets offered <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quicklinks-label-intranet/" title="Quicklinks: Bad Label or Indicative of Usability Issues"><em>Quicklinks</em></a>, or navigation shortcuts, to popular destinations. This convention was common 7 years ago when the first edition of the report was published and is still popular today. With so much information on intranets, shortcuts to content that belongs in lower levels of the hierarchy, but must be frequently accessed by employees, can be a big time saver. These shortcuts help employees discover new features, making the intranet more useful and interesting.</p>

<p>The best <em>Quicklinks</em> lists have <strong>descriptive names</strong>, such as <em>Most Popular Pages</em> or <em>Frequently Used Tools</em>, are placed <strong>prominently on the homepage</strong>, and are <strong>based on site analytics</strong>.</p>

<p>One way of <strong>engaging users</strong> is by showing what others are doing. <strong><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/social-proof-ux/" title="Social Proof in the User Experience">Social filters</a></strong> are popular on websites and increasingly on intranets as well.</p>

<p>Intranets in this report used the following social filters:</p>

<ul>

<li><em>Most Viewed</em></li>

<li><em>Trending #tags</em></li>

<li><em>Most popular docs</em></li>

<li><em>Top stories</em></li>

<li><em>Latest comments</em></li>

<li><em>Hot resources</em></li>

<li><em>Top <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/faqs-deliver-value/" title="FAQs Still Deliver Great Value">FAQs</a></em></li>

<li><em>Commonly Used Forms</em></li>

</ul>

<p>Some social filters encourage exploration, while others provide a shortcut to important information (like a <em>Quicklinks</em> list).</p>

<p>In addition to presenting the most popular pages (or documents, or tools), some intranets offered customizable <em>Quicklinks</em> lists. Customizability is a nice option, but users rarely engage with customization features. It’s time better spent to make the default presentation as useful as possible right out of the box.</p>

<p>Still, don’t forget the simplest, most-basic web navigation aid: <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wrong-information-scent-costs-sales/" title="Deceivingly Strong Information Scent Costs Sales">cross-reference links</a>. On many intranets, <em>Related Links</em> lists were immensely helpful, particularly when they were designed consistently, so users always knew where to find them and what to expect.</p>

<h2 id="toc-participating-organizations-8">Participating Organizations</h2>

<p>Our analysis encompassed a wide range of organizations in 13 countries:</p>

<ul>

<li>43 companies from a variety of industries, including financial services, utilities, and technology</li>

<li>14 government agencies</li>

<li>7 non-profits</li>

<li>6 healthcare providers</li>

<li>4 educational institutions</li>

</ul>

<p>Of the organizations, 9 were small (fewer than 500), 48 were mid-sized (500–20,000 employees), and 17 were large (more than 20,000 employees).</p>

<p>Note that 3 organizations participated in both editions: Cisco Systems, Inc.; Department for Work and Pensions (DWP); and Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL).</p>
