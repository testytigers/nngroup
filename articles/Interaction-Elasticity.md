<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Usage goes down as interaction costs increase. User motivation determines how fast demand drops, following an elasticity curve.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>People always want <strong> numeric rules</strong>:</p>

<ul>

<li>How many clicks should we have from the homepage to a product page?</li>

<li>How many links in a navigation menu?</li>

<li>How many seconds will users wait for a page to download before they leave?</li>

</ul>

<p>Sadly, there is <strong> no single number that answers </strong> such questions.</p>

<p>Yes, we know that <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/" title="Jakob Nielsen: Response Times - The 3 Important Limits "> response times</a> must be less than 1 second for <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/navigation-design/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: Full day seminar, IA2: Navigation Design"> navigation</a> to feel seamless and less than 10 seconds to prevent a user's attention from wandering. These time limits are caused by the human brain's structure and are thus firm and stable decade by decade (indeed, century by century). But does that mean that people will leave a site if its download times are above 1 or 10 seconds? No, it means that they're <em> more likely </em> to leave, because the slower a site is, the more unpleasant it is to use.</p>

<p>As for click and link counts, there's a reason we have a full-day seminar on <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/information-architecture/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: Full day seminar, Information Architecture 1: Structure Design"> how to structure a website</a>: These issues require substantial trade-off analyses to resolve correctly. The real question is not how <em> many </em> clicks or links, but how <em> easy </em> it is for users to pick the right one. A path with 5 easy clicks is vastly superior to one with 3 difficult clicks. And a menu with 10 easily understood items is better than a menu with 7 obscure ones.</p>

<p>The point is, you can't simply <em> count </em> without knowing what you're counting — you have to <em> understand </em><a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/hci/" title="Nielsen Norman Group, full-day training seminar: Principles of Interface Design"> usability principles</a> so that you can assess what's easy or difficult for your customers to use.</p>

<p>Still, shorter paths are usually better: 4 easy clicks are more usable than 5 easy clicks, because the extra click is more work for users. Similarly, shorter menus are faster to scan, assuming their category labels are clear and meaningful.</p>

<p>Most usability questions are not dichotomies. There's no simple cut-off where you're a 100% success on one side and a complete failure on the other. Rather, some designs are better than others and will drive more business.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-elasticity-demand-drops-as-cost-increases-1">Elasticity: Demand Drops as Cost Increases</h2>

<p>The field of economics provides an interesting analogy: that of <strong> price elasticity</strong>. Whatever your product, there's no single price point that will make all customers buy even though none would have paid a higher price. Instead, what happens is that the more you increase the price, the more customers turn away. Lower prices certainly drive sales, but you can't say that the price must be $x or nobody will buy.</p>

<p>Elasticity is the extent to which <strong> sales drop as prices increase. </strong> Demand might be either <strong> inelastic</strong>, meaning that people will buy at almost any price (and thus sales drop slowly as prices increase); or <strong> highly elastic</strong>, meaning that most people will buy only at very cheap prices (and thus sales drop fast if prices go up).</p>

<p><strong>Usability also exhibits elasticity</strong>. You can't say that response times must be less than x seconds or nobody will use the site. But, the faster the response times, the more people will use the site, and the more pages they'll visit.</p>

<p>Likewise, the more usability guidelines you follow, the more business you get. But you'll still get <em> some </em> business, even if your site blatantly violates key usability guidelines.</p>

<p>Earlier this year, I <a class="old" href="/articles/four-bad-designs/" title="Alertbox: Four Bad Designs"> analyzed four usability blunders</a>. All four were pretty bad, yet all four offending companies are still in business. The reason? They all do something well, and they therefore have some users who are sufficiently committed to make it through the design problems.</p>

<p>Similarly, if you have a good product, you can charge a high price because some people will willingly stretch their budgets to buy from you instead of from a cheaper competitor.</p>

<p><strong>The better you are, the less elasticity </strong> in the demand. Granted, your business will always drop off somewhat with higher prices or lower usability, but not as much as someone with a high-elasticity product.</p>

<p>Two things to note about interaction elasticity:</p>

<ul>

<li>If you're a <strong> high elasticity </strong> case, your usage will drop drastically with every minor degradation of the user experience.</li>

<li>Even if you have <strong> low elasticity </strong> (and thus highly committed users), why would you suffer <em> any </em> loss of business due to bad design? When it comes to price, it makes sense to charge more when you can, because you benefit from collecting more money for each sale. But with user interface design, you gain nothing when you reduce usability and antagonize your customers.</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-jumping-hurdles-2">Jumping Hurdles</h2>

<p><strong>Usability problems are like a barrier </strong> between you and your potential customers: the worse the design, the higher the barrier. Will users jump the hurdle? Some will, some won't. The lower your barrier and the more compelling your offer, the lower your interaction elasticity will be, and the more people will jump.</p>

<p>There are two strategies for increasing your website's business value: (1) do something that people <em> really </em> want and thus make demand less elastic, and (2) reduce <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/">interaction costs</a> by increasing usability. I would do both.</p>
