Aphone tree(also called anInteractive Voice-Response[IVR] system) generally consists of prerecorded audio messages that are presented when someone calls a business or organization. In most cases, callers select a number on the phone keypad associated with the recorded option most closely related to their question. In an increasing number of cases, callers can also summarize their question in a short word or phrase that the system will try to recognize in order to connect them with useful information.

We hope that every organization is striving to make its website as usable and informative as possible. However, many users will inevitably have questions that require a little extra help. An essential part of agood omnichannel experienceincludespositive interactions when a user is struggling and cannot succeed on their own.

These days, most organizations have so many users with so many of the same questions that it has become necessary to triage callers before they are connected to a live person. This is understandable given the time and cost required for employees to speak on the phone with hundreds (or thousands) of struggling people each day. Only very small organizations or those thatstill prioritize personal contact above all elsecan afford to immediately respond to every customer. While it may be efficient for the organization,a phone tree does not guarantee a positive experience for the caller.In fact, ask anyone you know about their most recent experience calling customer service and you will see what we mean.

Phone trees are older than the web. Early on, the phone was the easiest method for interacting with an organization. However, websites have become a much more convenient interaction channel to accomplish tasks for most users. Because the web is such a ubiquitous tool, there is little need for organizations to try and raise awareness of it by referring callers back to their websites.

With some exceptions, most callers have already visited an organization’s website or app by the time they call. They have either already attempted to answer their own question online or their question arosewhilethe user was completing an online task. Many users call only as a last resort because theinteraction costof making a phone call is much higher than simply searching for an answer on the web.

Ironically, many phone trees immediately direct users to a website or app with phrases such asOur agents do not have access to any more availability or information than what you see online or on our app”(recreation.gov) orMost requests can be done quicker and cheaper [sic] online. You can [make changes to your flight information] directly on our website. It's also quick and easy to book online or through our app(easyjet.com). While these statements may be true, they create a frustrating cycle asthe experience on the website forces users to call the organization for help and the phone tree tells them they should return to the website.

When was the last time you called an organization just to see what useful information the phone tree would provide? If you are like most people, you call only when you want to speak with a live person who can understand your problem and help you resolve it.The caller’s goal is to speak with a real person;therefore, the many layers of a phone tree can feel like a maze of obstacles separating the caller from their objective.

Many of these mazes are not accidental. Any organization that can resolve a caller’s issuewithoutpaying an employee to interact with that caller will save a significant amount of money over time.The organization’s goal is to avoid speaking with the callerand thus, directly conflicts with the caller’s goal.

When users don’t think a webpage is interesting or helpful, they generallyleave and visit another website— often that of a competitor. However, customers who call for help are not browsing and, in most cases, cannot simply call a different organization to resolve their question (especially if there is only one organization that provides that service). People generally call because they need to resolve a question that they can’t figure out in any other way —calling is their last option for help.To some extent, they are trapped.

Becauseusers highly value autonomy, they are likely to become frustrated and dissatisfied when they feel trapped — particularly if their phone-tree experience is confusing or difficult.

According to Stolovitch and Keeps (2011), an estimated83%of the information humans take in is through the eyes and only11%is through the ears. Information is much harder to process, remember, and understand when people don’t have anything to look at. The reason why most people self-identify as “visual learners” is that human brains benefit greatly from visual aids (a phenomenon well documented by thepicture-superiority effect).Interactions without visual elements — such as speaking on the phone or with anintelligent voice assistant— feel more abstract and put excessive strain on users’working memory.

One of the great benefits of a graphical user interface (GUI) isdirect manipulation, which allows people to visually reference information relevant to their taskrather than having to memorize it. GUIs also give usersdirect access to informationby allowing them to attend only to those parts of the interface that are relevant to their task. In contrast, like screen readers, phone trees present information sequentially — forcing people to listen to all available options (and remember at least some of them) in order to pick the correct one. In summary,callers are deprived of all visual aids and must memorize at least some of the information presentedto select the right option.

To better understand the usability of phone trees, we did anexpert reviewof phone trees from 22 organizations (listed at the end of this article).

The resulting recommendations will help user-experience designers improve phone trees for organizations of any size in any industry. These recommendations can be used as a checklist because they are based on the10 usability heuristics.

Bad example:The US Customs and Border Protection phone tree required callers to wait through 1 minute and 22 seconds of recorded English audio before presenting options for Spanish and Chinese. Even if the initial information was not available in Spanish, Spanish speakers should be able to select their language and bypass information they will not understand.

Good example:Once LG identified which appliance the caller had a question about, the phone tree saidSay yes now and I'll send you a link to get started.The link in the text message took the user to a page with information relevant to that appliance.

Bad example:Some phone trees inconsistently referred to the “#” symbol as both apound signand ahashat different points in the tree. If you are unsure how to refer to this symbol, we recommend choosing eitherpound(most common in the US) orhash(most common in the UK).Most important is that you use it consistently throughout your entire phone tree.

Bad example: The Intermountain Healthcare phone tree used the “#” sign to end the call. This was totally unnecessary, given that a caller could hang up at any time, and completely inconsistent with how most other phone trees utilize that symbol.

Bad example:When presenting the caller with seven options, the Customs and Border Protection phone tree started by presenting option “7,” then proceeded to option “1” and up from there ending with option “6.”

Bad example:Very few of the phone trees analyzed consistently allowed callers to undo selections. Even those trees that did allow callers to go backward at some points often failed to offer this ability in every menu and submenu. The best phone tree designs will remind the caller how they can return to the previous decision point as the last menu option at every level.

Bad example:On multiple levels in the phone tree, EasyJet provided only one menu option at a time and forced the caller to continue holding for more options: If you're calling for information about the latest travel restrictions, press 1. For anything else, please hold for more options; To check the status of your flight, press 1. Alternatively, please continue to hold for more options.

Good example:When Wells Fargo was unable to recognize what the caller was saying, the phone tree replied,I'm having trouble, so let's try using just your telephone keypad.While this is a good example of providing an alternative, this design would be even better if the option to use the keypad had been presented at the beginning along with the option to speak.

Partial good examples:The following two examples from the University of Edinburgh and University of Toronto each contained part of a good message. Combined, they would be even better:

You have called outside our office hours. Please call back between 9am and 5pm, Monday to Friday”(University of Edinburgh).

If you are calling to report an emergency situation, please press 5 now or dial 416-978-2222 to speak to the campus police. Otherwise, please visit our website at utoronto.ca where you will find detailed [information about] …(University of Toronto).

Bad example:Recreation.gov shared unnecessary promotional statements prior to providing any menu options: Looking for your next adventure at one of the nation's federal lands, waterways or monuments? Recreation.gov is here to help you dream up your trip, plan the details, and reserve experiences across the country to create lasting memories.

Partially good example:Intermountain Healthcare used the following statement at the beginning of the phone tree to help callers prepare information they will need to present:To help protect our patients’ privacy, please be prepared to validate your identification. Please be advised that only authorized callers will be able to receive patient information.(This statement would be even more helpful if the identification information that will be required was specified.)

Good example:Hilton allowed callers to indicate that they need more time and simply press “1” when they were ready to continue:

[phone tree] Say or enter your confirmation number followed by #. You can also say ‘I don't have it’ or ‘more time’ if you need time to find it.

[caller]More time

[phone tree] No problem. Take your time. When you are ready. Press 1 to continue.

Good example:UPS helped the caller find the tracking number of a package with the following statement:Your tracking number is usually on your package and most of them start with ‘1Z.’ The sender will usually provide it to you once the label has been created and it's ready to ship.

Good example:Delta Air Lines provided an update with the estimated amount of time the caller would still need to wait about every 2 minutes.

Bad example:Turkish Airlines interrupted the music about every 18 seconds. Over the course of 4 minutes and 25 seconds, the caller was interrupted 15 times with the phrases:Thank you for waiting, All of our customer representatives are currently speaking with other passengers,andYour estimated waiting time is XX minutes.

Conclusion

The usability of a phone tree relies on the same basic heuristics as a user interface. While phone trees have inherent limitations, making them as usable as possible will help callers more pleasantly and efficiently accomplish their tasks. Consider doing somebasic usability testingwith callers by giving them tasks and having them talk out loud as they navigate the phone tree. This type of testing is guaranteed to yield far better insights for improvement than asking callers to “stay on the line for a brief survey after the call has ended.”
