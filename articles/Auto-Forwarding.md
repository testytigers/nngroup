<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>The user&#x27;s target was at the top of the page in 98-point font. But she failed to find it because the panel auto-rotated instead of staying still.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>We recently ran a usability study in the U.K. where one user was attempting the following task: <em> "Does Siemens have any special deals on washing machines?" </em></p>

<p>Without much trouble, the user arrived at this homepage for Siemens Appliances in the U.K.:</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Screenshot of Siemens UK homepage for appliances, showing an automatic slide show that covers the top half of the visible page." loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2013/01/19/siemens-appliances-overlooked-promotion_.png" style="width: 600px; height: 464px;"/>

<figcaption><em>User task: "Does Siemens have any special deals on washing machines?" </em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>What do you think? Could the user answer the question? Given that the biggest item on the homepage — in both screen space and font size — is an offer of £100 off "selected Siemens appliances" next to a large photo of a washing machine, you would think so. For good measure, the same offer is featured in a small promotion tile in the right margin.</p>

<p>Nonetheless, <strong>the design failed for this user task</strong>. After an extended visit to the website — including much time scrutinizing this homepage — the user gave up and assumed that Siemens didn't have any special deals for her. (Her concluding comment? <em> "I wouldn't choose </em> [a Siemens appliance] <em> unless I was very rich." </em> )</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-small-promo-box-ignored-1">Small Promo Box: Ignored</h2>

<p>We can <strong> easily dismiss the small promotion </strong> on the right. We don't even need a test to know that<strong> most users will ignore this design element </strong> for three reasons:</p>

<ol>

<li><strong><a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings/" title="Alertbox: Banner Blindness, Old and New Findings">Banner blindness</a></strong>: This was the most important problem. The promotion looks so much like an ad that users are bound to ignore it.</li>

<li><strong>Location</strong>: <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/horizontal-attention-leans-left/" title="Alertbox: Horizontal Attention Leans Left">Most user attention focuses on the left </a> side of web pages. <strong>Placement in the right column drastically reduces the number of users who will see the item</strong>.</li>

<li><strong>Terrible ad copy:</strong> The wording is<strong> user-repelling</strong>. Yes, there's a nice big "£100" attention grabber, but the main copy is "Rewarding.Life.Style." Even if users do look (unlikely), few will click the box. This <strong> is content-free content </strong> to at least 99% of humans outside Siemens' marketing department. (Granted, I never wrote a column about the guideline that copywriting should make sense, but I thought it was self-evident. For an analysis of an often-tortured writing genre, see my discussion of <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/tagline-blues-whats-the-site-about/" title="Alertbox: Tagline Blues: What's the Site About?"> taglines</a>.)</li>

</ol>

<h2 id="toc-big-carousel-like-accordion-ignored-2">Big Carousel-Like Accordion: Ignored</h2>

<p>As the above screenshot shows, the page's big box has two points against it: it uses <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/fancy-formatting-looks-like-an-ad/" title="Alertbox: Fancy Formatting, Fancy Words = Looks Like a Promotion = Ignored"><strong> fancy formatting</strong> (which is often ignored)</a>, and it <strong>looks somewhat like a banner</strong>.</p>

<p>However, to identify the real conversion killer, you need one more clue that can't be deduced from a static screenshot:</p>

<ul>

<li>The box looks like an accordion, but it acts more like a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designing-effective-carousels/" title="Carousel Usability: Designing an Effective UI for Websites with Content Overload">carousel</a> because it <strong> auto-rotates to show a new panel </strong> every 5 seconds.</li>

</ul>

<p>Deadly. Accordions and carousels should <strong> show a new panel only when users ask for it</strong>. Otherwise, it should <strong> stand still </strong> and let users read the information in peace, without having the rug yanked from under them. As our user said about the Siemens big rotating box: <em> "I didn't have time to read it. It keeps flashing too quickly." </em></p>

<p>Auto-forwarding causes many usability problems:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Moving UI elements usually reduce <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/accessibility/" title="List of articles about accessibility"> accessibility</a></strong>, particularly for users with motor skill issues who have difficulty clicking something before it's taken away.</li>

<li><strong><a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/writing-for-lower-literacy-users/" title="Alertbox: Lower-Literacy Users: Writing for a Broad Consumer Audience">Low-literacy users</a> often don't have enough time to read </strong>the information before it's removed.</li>

<li><strong><a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-international-purchasers/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: E-Commerce UX Vol. 10, International Purchasers">International users</a> also read more slowly</strong> if your site is not in their native language, and thus they won't be able to understand a panel if it's displayed only briefly.</li>

<li><strong>Single-item visibility is reduced by having to take turns being on display</strong>. The probability that users will spot the item they want is drastically reduced when only one thing is displayed at any given time; in the Siemens example, the discount deal is visible only 20% of the time.</li>

<li><strong>It's just plain annoying for users to lose control</strong> of the user interface when things move around of their own accord.</li>

<li>Most important, because it moves, users automatically <strong> assume that it might be an advertisement</strong>, which makes them more likely to ignore it.</li>

</ul>

<p>In my example, the user didn't see the £100 offer during her initial review of the top of the page. She then scrolled down a bit, and never saw the full box during her remaining time on the homepage.</p>

<p>The big box's dysfunctional design is further impaired by an even worse use of content-free content than the small box. In the accordion, the meaningless phrase <em>Rewarding.Life.Style</em> is used as the link text for the button to show the discount offer.</p>

<p>Although worthless word count is always wasteful, given <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="Alertbox: How Little Do Users Read?"> how little people read on a website</a>, it's downright <strong> disastrous to use worthless words for a link</strong>. <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/" title="Alertbox: Information Foraging: Why Google Makes People Leave Your Site Faster"> Information scent</a> is one of the main determinants of successful navigation: people only click links that (a) make sense and (b) describe something they want.</p>

<p>This example design is flawed in so many ways, but the worst offense is probably in <strong>making something move that should be static</strong>. It's sad to think that extra money was wasted on jazzing up this design with harmful moving features, rather than just creating simple content that clearly communicates the company's value proposition. </p>
