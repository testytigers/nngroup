<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Bystanders rated mobile-phone conversations as dramatically more noticeable, intrusive, and annoying than conversations conducted face-to-face. While volume was an issue, hearing only half a discussion also seemed to up the irritation factor.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p><a class="out" href="http://www-users.york.ac.uk/~am1/" title="Personal homepage">Andrew Monk </a> and colleagues from the University of York have performed a wonderful study to assess why it's so annoying when other people have cellphone conversations in public.</p>

<p>The researchers staged one-minute conversations in front of <strong> unsuspecting commuters </strong> who were either riding a train or waiting for a bus. In half the cases, two actors conversed face-to-face while seated next to a potential test participant. In the other half, a single actor talked on a mobile phone while seated next to a potential participant.</p>

<p>Furthermore, the actors conducted half of the conversations at a <strong> normal loudness level </strong> , whereas the other half were <strong> exaggeratedly loud </strong> (as measured on a volume meter). The actual content and duration of the conversations were the same in all conditions.</p>

<p>After each test conversation, researchers approached the bystanders and asked them to complete a small survey about the conversation. In other words, while the conversation was taking place, the <strong> participants didn't know that they were part of an experiment </strong> , but rather assumed that the conversation was the normal behavior of one or two other commuters.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-the-consent-question-1">The Consent Question</h2>

<p>Some people might question the <strong> ethics </strong> of conducting research without first acquiring participants' informed consent, but in my view, this was a perfectly ethical study. The real question is whether you're harming participants, not whether you're pushing enough paper around to satisfy a bureaucracy.</p>

<p>In this case, it would have been impossible to have people first sign a statement that they would observe a conversation and then comment on it. Knowing about the conversation in advance would completely change the participants' experience. Also, in this study, if a person didn't want to participate, all they had to do was refuse to answer the questions. Overhearing a short conversation in public is a common phenomenon; it does no harm and falls within the ethical guidelines for usability studies.</p>

<p>(The ethics of usability is one of the topics covered in my 3-day camp teaching <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/events/tutorials/camp.html" title="Tutorial description and course outline"> Usability in Practice </a> at the <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/events/" title="Conference program and list of usability training tutorials"> Usability Week 2009 conference </a> in Washington DC, San Francisco, London, and Sydney. )</p>

<p> </p>

<p>Clearly, mobile phones score far worse than face-to-face conversations, confirming much anecdotal evidence. As we might expect, loud conversations score worse than quieter conversations. It's striking, however, that mobile-phone conversations are judged more negatively than loud conversations. Participants even said that the <em> volume </em> of the mobile-phone conversations was more annoying than those that occurred face-to-face, even though the volume was the same, and was controlled by objective measures.</p>

<h2 id="toc-can-we-make-mobile-phones-less-annoying-3">Can We Make Mobile Phones Less Annoying?</h2>

<p>The researchers asked test participants to rate how annoyed they were by the mobile phone's <strong> ring tone </strong> . (No comparable question applied to the face-to-face condition.) However, people didn't find the ring to be particularly bad, so the fact that mobile phones ring doesn't seem to explain why bystanders hate mobile-phone conversations.</p>

<p>Speech volume did affect bystanders' annoyance level: loud phone conversations were judged more negatively than phone conversations conducted in a normal voice. Designing phones that <strong> encourage users to speak softly </strong> will reduce their impact on other people. For example, more sensitive microphones and improved quality on incoming audio will make most users less inclined to shout.</p>

<p>But loudness wasn't the worst problem with mobile phones. In fact, even phone conversations in a normal voice received worse scores than face-to-face conversations. The worst problem seems to be that conversations on mobile phones are more noticeable than face-to-face conversations. This seems odd, since two people talking together project twice the amount of audio as one person talking on the phone.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, Monk and his colleagues don't provide the final answer; more research is called for. But the problem seems to be that <strong> people pay more attention when they hear only half a conversation </strong> . It's apparently easier to tune out the continuous drone of a complete conversation, in which two people take turns speaking, than it is to ignore a person speaking and falling silent in turns.</p>

<p>Based on this early research, it's hard to know how we might design phones that overcome the turn-taking problem in audio projection. Speakerphones might be an answer, but I don't think so.</p>

<p>What is certain is that the research documents the fact that mobile phones <em> are </em> annoying, and that conversation loudness is only one factor. If mobile-phone vendors want to <strong> avoid a backlash </strong> against their products, they're well advised to heed these findings and launch a major effort to make mobile phones less irritating to bystanders.</p>

<h2 id="toc-methodology-advances-4">Methodology Advances</h2>

<p>Even though these findings raise more questions than they answer, I find this study interesting for two reasons.</p>

<ul>

<li>It pioneers the study of <strong> bystander usability </strong> . It's not enough to test usability with a design's direct user. We must also consider those who are getting a "user experience" whether they want it or not. This question will become more important as user interfaces leave the screen behind and become physical and/or mobile.</li>

<li>It's a great example of <strong> field research </strong> and of collecting participant data in a naturalistic context. Staging scenarios with actors is an unusual method in usability research, but such methods might become more common as we move into testing collaborative systems and environmental designs.</li>

</ul>
