</div><h2 id="toc-people-rely-on-links-to-the-homepage-1">People Rely on Links to the Homepage</h2>

<p>A direct, one-click link to the homepage is a must-have on websites. There are several reasons why:</p>

<ul>

<li>Going back to the homepage is a <strong>common task</strong>. People often go to the homepage when they’re disoriented, they have gone too deep into a site, or they’re ready to start a new task.</li>

<li>Because of their dependency on <strong>search</strong> engines, most users enter websites through an interior page and bypass the homepage entirely. Easy access to the homepage provides a new starting point for those who are on the wrong page or want to explore other parts of the site.</li>

</ul>

<p>There are two ways to present links to homepage:  <strong>implicit</strong>, through logos, and <strong>explicit</strong>, through a link labeled <em>Home </em>or similar.  <strong>You should use both these types of links.</strong> Below are guidelines for their use.</p>

<h2 id="toc-guidelines-for-implicit-links-to-the-homepage-2">Guidelines for Implicit Links to the Homepage</h2>

<h3>Website Logos Should Link to the Homepage</h3>

<p>​This recommendation is a long-standing web convention. Over time through trial and error, many people have learned that clicking on a site’s logo leads them back to the homepage. Following this standard on your site reduces confusion by matching your UI to users’ expectations.</p>

<p>However, having logos link to the homepage is not foolproof. Although it is a well-established convention, it is <em>not </em>understood by all users, especially less technical audiences. In usability studies we <em>still</em> observe people struggling to find links to the homepage, especially when logos are not placed in the standard top-left positioning or they look like regular text.</p>

<h3>Place the Logo on the Top Left of the Page</h3>

<p>The left-aligned logo is the most familiar placement, and is where users look to find it. This expectation is so strong that veering from this learned pattern can cause significant harm.</p>

<p>Our research indicates <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/centered-logos/">logos positioned in nonstandard locations damage usability</a>.  Users are 6 times more likely to successfully navigate to the homepage in one click when the logo is left-aligned than when it is centered. Simply moving the logo towards the right side of the page caused people to make mistakes in finding logos, or even worse, to not find them at all. (Furthermore, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/logo-placement-brand-recall/">right-aligned logos hurt brand recall</a>.)</p>

<h3>The Logo Should Look Different than Other Page Text</h3>

<p>If the logo looks like a navigation option or like headings on the page, it can be harder to locate. Help users find the logo by making it stand out visually.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="536" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/amgen-science.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>Amgen.com: Placing the logo among the navigational links decreases the probability that people will find it, especially when the visual design of the logo makes it blend in with the other text links.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="368" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/gm.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>GM.com: The </em>General Motors<em> at the top of the page looks more like a heading than a logo. And clicking on the hamburger menu does not yield a link to the homepage.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Having a logo positioned in the proper place does not produce flawless results. While it’s good to left-align logos, you’ll need to do more to make homepage navigation transparent.</p>

<h2 id="toc-guidelines-for-explicit-links-to-the-homepage-3">Guidelines for Explicit Links to the Homepage</h2>

<h3>Include a Link Labeled <em>Home</em></h3>

<p>To minimize homepage-navigation confusion, offer both clickable logos <em>and</em> actual <em>Home</em> links. These <em>Home</em> links can be presented in places such as the site’s global navigation or in breadcrumbs. Positioning them towards the top left of the page (above the content) makes them the most discoverable. Placing them too far from this area may cause users to miss them, especially if your site is visually busy.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="416" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/chemence.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>Chemence.com:  People can easily find the link to the homepage. The logo is placed in the top-left corner and an actual </em>Home<em> link is also present. Although the position of this link is far from the top-left corner, it is s acceptable because the page is sparse, with few distracting elements.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="255" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/newbalancebreadcrumb.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>NewBalance.com: Having Home listed in the breadcrumbs is an effective way to provide users direct access to the homepage, provided that the breadcrumbs appear on all site pages.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>Start Breadcrumbs with the <em>Home</em> Link</h3>

<p>Breadcrumbs tell people their current location while offering one-click access to the higher site levels. The best practice is to start the breadcrumb trail with the <em>Home</em> link as it represents the highest level.</p>

<h3>If You Use Breadcrumbs, Don’t Show <em>Home</em> in the Main Navigation</h3>

<p>There’s no need to offer <em>Home</em> links in both breadcrumbs and the site’s navigation. Having the same link twice on the same page is a bad idea because it increases complexity needlessly.  Duplicating links is one of the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/navigation-cognitive-strain/">four major dangerous navigation techniques</a> that cause cognitive strain.</p>

<h3>Don’t Include an Active <em>Home</em> Link on the Homepage</h3>

<p>It’s confusing to have an active link to any given page on that page itself, and the homepage is no exception. Offer <em>Home </em>links only on interior pages. On the homepage, either clearly show <em>Home</em> in a selected state or do not present the <em>Home</em> link at all.</p>

<h3>Distinguish Between <em>Home</em> Links to a Subsite Homepage and <em>Home </em>Links to the Site Homepage</h3>

<p>In general, it’s best to name the link to a website’s homepage <em>Home</em>. However, large organizations that have <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/universal-navigation/">subsites that connect to the main website</a> may have different homepages. On subsites, make it easy for people to gain access to the official website by making the link to the site’s main homepage distinguishable.</p>

<p>In most cases, using different labels for the home links to the various homepages (subsites vs. official site) will help.  As with all links, provide as much <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/">information scent</a> as needed to help users disambiguate the meaning of the link. In some situations, an abbreviated URL (e.g., “site.com”) may be enough, while in others you may need to be more explicit and include additional explanations.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="496" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/stanford.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>Biology.Standford.edu: The Department of Biology website clearly labels its homepage while offering a clear link to the university’s homepage.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="" height="337" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2017/06/09/lakers-home.jpg" width="1000"/>

<figcaption><em>NBA.com/Lakers: The Lakers basketball team site displays a clear link to the official National Basketball Association website (</em>NBA.com<em>).</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-conclusion-4">Conclusion</h2>

<p>It is great if your site logo offers a one-click approach to navigating to your homepage. But don’t stop there. While more people than ever have learned to click on the logo to go to the homepage, many users still look for an actual <em>Home</em> link.</p>
