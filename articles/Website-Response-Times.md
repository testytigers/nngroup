<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Slow page rendering today is typically caused by server delays or overly fancy page widgets, not by big images. Users still hate slow sites and don&#x27;t hesitate telling us.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Users <em> really </em> care about speed in interaction design. In 1997, I wrote a column called "<a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-need-for-speed-1997/" title="Alertbox: The Need for Speed">The Need for Speed</a>," pointing out how much users hated slow-loading web pages. Back then, big images were the main cause of response-time delays, and our guideline recommended that you keep images small.</p>

<p>Today, most people (in some countries) have broadband, so you might think that download times are no longer a usability concern. And yes, actual <strong> image download is rarely an issue </strong> for today's users (though images can still cause delays on <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-usability-update/" title="Alertbox: The State of Mobile User Experience"> mobile devices</a>).</p>

<p>Still, response times are as relevant as ever. That's because responsiveness is a basic user interface design rule that's dictated by human needs, not by individual technologies. In a client usability study we just completed, for example, users complained that "<em>it's being a little slow.</em>"</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-speed-matters-1">Speed Matters</h2>

<p>Responsiveness matters for two reasons:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Human limitations</strong>, especially in the areas of memory and attention (as further discussed in our seminar on <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/human-mind/" title="Nielsen Norman Group training seminar: detailed course description"> The Human Mind and Usability</a>). We simply don't perform as well if we have to wait and suffer the inevitable decay of information stored in short-term memory.</li>

<li><strong>Human aspirations</strong>. We like to feel in control of our destiny rather than subjugated to a computer's whims. Also, when companies make us wait instead of providing responsive service, they seem either arrogant or incompetent.</li>

</ul>

<p><strong>A snappy user experience beats a glamorous one</strong>, for the simple reason that people <strong> engage more </strong> with a site when they can move freely and focus on the content instead of on their endless wait.</p>

<p>In a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-branding/" title="Brand Is Experience in the Digital Age">recent study</a>, we asked users what they thought about various websites they had used in the past. So, their responses were based not on immediate use (as in normal usability studies), but on whatever <em> past </em> experiences were strong enough to form memories. Under these conditions, it was striking to hear users complain about the slowness of certain sites. Slowness (or speed) makes such an impact that it can become one of the brand values customers associate with a site. (Obviously, "sluggish" is not a brand value that any marketing VP would actively aim for, but the actual experience of using a site is more important than slogans or advertising in forming customer impressions of a brand.)</p>

<p>Indeed, we <strong> get findings related to website speed </strong> almost every time we run a study. When sites shave as little as 0.1 seconds off response time, the outcome is a juicy lift in <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/conversion-rates/" title="Definition: Conversion Rates">conversion rates</a>. Today or the 1990s? Same effect.</p>

<h2 id="toc-response-time-limits-2">Response-Time Limits</h2>

<p>The <a class="old" href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/" title="Response Times: The 3 Important Limits"> 3 response-time limits</a> are the same today as when I wrote about them in 1993 (based on 40-year-old research by human factors pioneers):</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>0.1 seconds </strong> gives the feeling of <strong> instantaneous </strong> response — that is, the outcome feels like it was caused by the user, not the computer. This level of responsiveness is essential to support the feeling of <strong><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/direct-manipulation/" title="Definition: Direct Manipulation"> direct manipulation</a> </strong> (direct manipulation is one of the key GUI techniques to increase user engagement and control — for more about it, see our <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/hci/" title="Nielsen Norman Group usability training tutorial: detailed course outline"> User Interface Principles Every Designer Must Know</a> course).</li>

<li><strong>1 second </strong> keeps the user's flow of thought <strong> seamless</strong>. Users can sense a delay, and thus know the computer is generating the outcome, but they still feel in control of the overall experience and that they're moving freely rather than waiting on the computer. This degree of responsiveness is needed for good <a class="new" href="https://www.nngroup.com/topic/navigation/" title="Navigation Design and Information Architecture: Nielsen Norman Group articles, reports, and courses"> navigation</a>.</li>

<li><strong>10 seconds </strong> keeps the user's <strong> attention</strong>. From 1–10 seconds, users definitely feel at the mercy of the computer and wish it was faster, but they can handle it. After 10 seconds, they start thinking about other things, making it harder to get their brains back on track once the computer finally does respond.</li>

</ul>

<p>A 10-second delay will often make users <strong> leave a site </strong> immediately. And even if they stay, it's harder for them to understand what's going on, making it less likely that they'll succeed in any difficult tasks.</p>

<p>Even a few seconds' delay is enough to create an <strong> unpleasant </strong> user experience. Users are no longer in control, and they're consciously annoyed by having to wait for the computer. Thus, with repeated short delays, users will give up unless they're extremely committed to completing the task. The result? You can easily lose half your sales (to those less-committed customers) simply because your site is a few seconds too slow for each page.</p>

<h2 id="toc-fancy-widgets-sluggish-response-3">Fancy Widgets, Sluggish Response</h2>

<p>Instead of big images, today's big response-time sinners are typically overly <strong> complex data processing </strong> on the server or <strong> overly fancy widgets </strong> on the page (or <em> too many </em> fancy widgets).</p>

<p>Here's an example from a recent <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/topic/eyetracking/" title="Eyetracking articles, reports, and courses - Nielsen Norman Group">eyetracking study</a> we conducted to generate new material for our <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/courses/web-page-design/" title="Nielsen Norman Group training seminar: detailed course description">training course on Web Page UX Design</a>. The following gaze plots show two different users' behavior on the same page, which contained a slideshow widget in the top yellow box that required <strong> 8 seconds to download</strong>:</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Eyetracking gaze plots of two different users viewing the same page." height="665" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/slow-widget-not-seen.png" width="383"/>

<figcaption><em>Gaze plots from two different users: the blue dots indicate where users looked (one fixation per dot).</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>The test participant in the top gaze plot fixated a few times within the big empty color block before the content downloaded, then spent the remaining time looking at the rest of the page. This user <strong> never looked at the big promotional space </strong> after it had rendered.</p>

<p>The second user (bottom gaze plot) happened to be looking away from the screen during the 8 seconds when the promotional content downloaded. Thus, the first time he looked at the page he saw it as intended, complete with the entire promo.</p>

<p>The slideshow occupies <strong> 23% of the page</strong>, not counting a footer that's not shown here. The user who had to endure the download delay spent only <strong> 1% of her total viewing time </strong> within this space. In contrast, the user who in effect received instantaneous page rendering (because he didn't look until it was done), spent <strong> 20% of his viewing time </strong> within the slideshow area.</p>

<p>Although 8 seconds might not seem like a big delay, it's enough to kill this big promo that the company's web team probably spent weeks designing. If they had allocated the space to something that rendered in 1 second instead of 8, they would have achieved much better results.</p>

<h2 id="toc-different-causes-same-effect-4">Different Causes, Same Effect</h2>

<p>Response times are a matter of user experience: How much time does it take before the computer is ready to serve the user? The reasons behind delays don't matter to users. All they know is that they're getting poor service, which is annoying.</p>

<p>Big images in 1997. Slow servers or overly fancy widgets in 2010. Same effect. Make it snappy, and you'll have a big leg up on the competition and their slow sites.</p>
