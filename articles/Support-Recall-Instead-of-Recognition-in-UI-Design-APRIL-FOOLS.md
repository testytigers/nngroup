Since 1994, one of the10 usability heuristicshas been that user-interface design shouldsupport recognition rather than recall. The argument used to be that it’s easier for people to recognize that something shown by the computer is what they want (say, a command in a menu) than to dredge out of memory the name of that something.

Today we are announcing that this guideline should be reversed for modern user-interface design.Now, recall is better than recognition.It’s not so bad to have to change one of our 10 heuristics after 28 years. 90% of the heuristics still remain valid, which is the same as we have found inpast analyses of old usability guidelines.

Mostly, what’s good design remains the same decade after decade, but it’s reasonable that a guideline that refers to something as changeable as human memory can — and thus should — change.

Why is recall now preferable to recognition in user-interface design? Because users have changed.

Decades of good UX in which designers have followed our recommendations (including the one favoring recognition over recall) have trained users to not use their memory skills and have made them lazy. The result is people who are incapable to remember simple things like 8×9=72 or that Copenhagen is the capital of Denmark or that Ctrl-S saves a document.

Moreover, users are getting older, and we know from ourresearch with seniorsthat, while there are many differences between old and young people, one of the most salient ones is that old people have weak memories. If you have never forgotten where you left the car keys, that proves one of two things: either you don’t own a car or you have not yet passed 50 years of age.

What can we do to fight the decay of memory proficiency and prevent an epidemic of memory loss? The answer is to force people to use their memory more in order to strengthen it. Exercising memory skills will help today’s young users preserve their memory abilities into old age and will also let older users retain whatever skills they have left.

Addressing the issue of memory degradation will not only serve humanity, but it will also be important for the world economy, since more and more workers are getting older. If we can help old employees remember better, we will add millions to the bottom lines of most companies. Thus, the new guideline is even more important for thedesign of intranetsandcomplex enterprise applications.

Since we only just reversed the heuristic today, we don’t yet have enough research to provide a full set of design guidelines for strengthening users’ memory skills. However, prior research onworking memoryprovides some ideas for further progress. For example, we know that distractor tasks reduce the number of slots available in working memory to support the primary task. To build on this finding, you could ask usability-study participants to remember 3 random digits while they use your design. If this degrades users’ performance, you have successfully designed a product that taxes people’s memory limitations, which means that it will be a perfect user experience to help people get better in the long run.
