<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>300,000 words of usability essays have had an impact: online user interfaces are considerably easier to use now than they were in 1995. Many predictions and recommendations have come true, though the full Alertbox vision is far from realized.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>It seems like I wrote "<a class="old" href="/articles/alertbox-5-years-retrospective" title="Alertbox May 2000">Alertbox Five Years Retrospective</a>" just yesterday, but another five years have passed. Since writing my first column in June 1995, I've published 247 columns comprising almost <strong> 300,000 words</strong>. That's a lot of writing and a lot of content to give away on the Internet. Has it been worth the effort?</p>

<p>My most-read article, "<a class="old" href="http://www.useit.com/alertbox/9605.html">Top Ten Mistakes of Web Design</a>," has long passed two million readers. The average Alertbox gets 300,000 page views; the total number of page views for all columns is about 50 million. Even if I never wrote another article, the Alertbox would be a 100-million-page-view project, because the next ten years will double the readership of the archived pages.</p>

<p>Yes: <em> it was worth it. </em></p>

<p>When I conducted my first user tests of websites and intranets in 1994, I was probably the only person in the world with this esoteric interest. Web people didn't care about usability, and usability people didn't care about the Web. After years of incessantly promoting user research findings for websites and intranets, the situation has changed: thousands of people now work on online usability. Nielsen Norman Group alone has <strong> trained 11,208 people </strong> and, given that many other places teach usability as well, the worldwide total is no doubt much larger.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-milestones-1">Milestones</h2>

<p>I included cartoons in the <a class="old" href="/articles/top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-2002"> Top Ten Mistakes of 2002</a>. It was challenging (but fun) to come up with illustrations for often-abstract usability principles. Even with concrete, visual principles, coming up with a humorous cartoon was sometimes tough, as when trying to illustrate "small font sizes" in a readable manner.</p>

<p>In addition to mainstream user coverage, my column content expanded to include findings from studies of <a class="old" href="/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues/" title="Alertbox: Kids' Corner -- Website Usability for Children"> children</a>, <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers" title="Alertbox: Usability of Websites for Teenagers"> teenagers</a>, <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-for-senior-citizens" title="Alertbox: Usability for Senior Citizens"> senior citizens</a>, users with <a class="old" href="/articles/beyond-accessibility-treating-users-with-disabilities-as-people" title="Alertbox: Beyond Accessibility: Treating Users with Disabilities as People"> disabilities</a>, and <a class="old" href="/articles/writing-for-lower-literacy-users" title="Alertbox: Lower-Literacy Users"> low-literacy users</a>. Each group requires special considerations; as the Internet matures, designers must recognize that most users are not just like them.</p>

<p>I reached beyond websites and intranets to cover <strong> email usability</strong>, including both <a class="old" href="/articles/e-mail-newsletters-usability" title="Alertbox: Targeted Email Newsletters Show Continued Strength"> email newsletters</a> and <a class="old" href="/articles/transactional-and-confirmation-email" title="Alertbox: Automated Customer Service Email and Transactional Messages"> confirmation emails</a>. I also covered many non-online topics, from <a class="old" href="/articles/why-consumer-products-have-bad-ux" title="Alertbox: Why Consumer Products Have Inferior User Experience"> consumer electronics</a> to <a class="old" href="/articles/in-the-future-well-all-be-harry-potter" title="Alertbox: In the Future, We'll All Be Harry Potter"> Harry Potter</a> .</p>

<p>Of course, I continued covering many themes from the first five years, including an emphasis on simplicity, user empowerment, and <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-101-introduction-to-usability" title="Alertbox: Usability 101: Introduction to Usability"> usability methodology</a>.</p>

<h2 id="toc-predicting-the-long-tail-2">Predicting "The Long Tail"</h2>

<p>In 1997, I wrote the " <a class="old" href="/articles/do-websites-have-increasing-returns"> Do Websites Have Increasing Returns?</a>" column, discussing the relative value of big and small websites. I predicted that <strong> small sites would generate 75% of the Web's total value </strong> because they can be more targeted than big sites.</p>

<p>Admittedly, my predictions weren't always 100% accurate: I assumed we'd have 100 million websites by now, and we still have "only" 64 million. But, my main point has now become received wisdom: the "<a class="old" href="http://www.thelongtail.com" title="Chris Anderson about The Long Tail">long tail</a>" meme states that the aggregate sales of non-bestsellers sum to much more than the highly visible business done by the few top hits.</p>

<p>Most current discussions of the long tail underestimate the non-hits and assume that each point on the curve has the same value. But on the Web, being small means that you can better <strong> target your content </strong> and thus provide higher value per unit than more generic services.</p>

<p>In a follow-up Alertbox, "<a class="old" href="/articles/diversity-is-power-for-specialized-sites">Diversity is Power for Specialized Sites</a>" (2003), I returned to this second part of my argument and emphasized the treasure found in online niches.</p>

<p>I should be happy that half of my position has become widely accepted and the driving force for heavy VC investments today. But there's still too much commotion about aggregators, and too little interest in niche services in their own right.</p>

<h2 id="toc-how-much-progress-in-a-decade-3">How Much Progress in a Decade?</h2>

<p>Taking a widescreen view of the past ten years, there is no doubt that the Web is <a class="new" href="/articles/progress-in-usability-fast-or-slow" title="Alertbox: Progress in Usability - Fast or Slow?"> making usability progress</a>:</p>

<ul>

<li>Blatant design <strong> stupidities </strong> like splash screens are pretty much history.</li>

<li>Usability <strong> metrics </strong> are trending up, whether we measure success rates or guideline compliance.</li>

<li>Many companies now have <strong> dedicated usability specialists </strong> on staff, and even more companies say that they want to improve the usability of their websites and intranets.</li>

<li>Internet managers have become more willing to <strong> follow design conventions </strong> rather than flout them and thus annoy their users.</li>

</ul>

<p>A decade is not much time in the great scheme of things, yet there has been significant movement in the direction I've argued for.</p>

<p>And, yes, there have been <strong> disappointments</strong>. Success rates increase by about 4% per year and now stand at 66% across corporate websites. Given this, eight years remain before we'll get truly usable websites. In addition, website quality level is horrendous relative to the standards of <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-and-six-sigma" title="Alertbox: Two Sigma -- Usability and Six Sigma Quality Assurance"> six sigma</a> or any other industrial-strength quality engineering process.</p>

<p>When I was <strong> wrong</strong>, it was often because I was too <strong> enthusiastic </strong> about a new technology's potential. When I was <strong> right</strong>, it was often because I was <strong> conservative</strong>. One of my bigger mistakes was to expect rapid progress in mobile services. <a class="old" href="/articles/wap-mobile-phones-field-study-findings" title="Alertbox: WAP Field Study Findings"> WAP </a>was a deserved flop, but I expected the next generation to be better and more widely used in its wake.</p>

<p>Finally, there's a reason the old "top ten mistakes" articles continue to be popular: despite clear documentation, website designers continue to make these mistakes at an astounding rate. Sad. But at least the mistakes are not as common anymore. We really are making progress. Even so, I'll have plenty of fodder for hundreds of columns to come.</p>
