Affinity diagramming(also known as affinity mapping or the KJ method) is an effective technique for organizing and synthesizing large amounts of data.

In broad strokes, in an affinity-diagramming workshop, individual items, such as feature ideas or user-research observations, are each written onto a different sticky note, and all the sticky notes are placed onto a wall. A team then works together to cluster related sticky notes, eventually giving groups descriptive names. The end result is a set of groups.

In many UX scenarios, affinity diagramming is followed by a prioritization exercise to determine which group needs to be addressed first.

While affinity diagramming seems quite simple and straightforward, it is often done wrong. The reality is that it takes time and skill both to facilitate and to participate in an affinity-diagramming workshop. There are several pitfalls that impact the validity of results.

When I first started facilitating affinity-diagramming sessions, I would explain how the process of sorting worked, and I would pause to see if the team had any questions. Typically, there were none — sorting seems relatively simple in theory. Then, I would start a timer and turn participants loose on the wall. But rather than diving into a flurry of sorting, the participants would stare at the wall, blank faced, unsure where to start. Eventually, one timid hand would go up, followed by the question, “Wait, so what are we sorting these based on?”

Often, “sort these based on theme” is not a sufficient instruction. There are many arguably valid ways of sorting a pile of items.

After this happened a few times, I learned the importance of starting with a “focus question.” Choosing the right focus question is critical, as it can dramatically change how the data will get sorted.

Keyword matching is a concept that emerges fromcard-sorting methodology(a process with mechanics nearly identical to affinity-diagramming, though with different aims). It refers to the sorting of sticky notes based on keywords that they share.

For example, let’s take again our food-related stickies, and let’s imagine that we are indeed a grocery store attempting to determine where to stock items. A team engaged in keyword matching might placeApplesandApplecider and put those together. A particularly careless team might even addPineapplesto that pile.

Keyword matching often leads to superficial groupings that end up not being useful to the team going through the exercise. There is no easy way to get around it. (In theory, you could change the wording of the stickies to avoid common words, but that is often not realistic.)

That said, I do have two methods that I use to minimize keyword matching.

Facilitators must always be on the lookout for the emergence ofgroupthinkin UX workshops. The primary benefit of a workshop derives from the diversity of opinions from different roles and backgrounds. If an overly vocal minority starts to impact the mindset of the team, that benefit is drastically reduced.

Often, one of these types of people will interject early on in the clustering process, perhaps with a well-meaning suggestion for the most efficient method of sorting the sticky notes. Often, other participants will fall in line, leading to the dreaded groupthink.

This is a problem because one of the intended benefits of affinity diagramming comes from the initial period of “organized chaos,” in which different people start sorting using different strategies and frameworks. Eventually, the most relevant or successful of these strategies will rise to the top organically. If the team falls in line behind one framework from the outset, other superior strategies will never be given the space to emerge.

Luckily, the solution to this is simple (though sometimes difficult to enforce): silence. Explain to the participants that the clustering should be done in silence. Warn people that the process will feel challenging and possibly chaotic, but that working in silence is part of the requisite journey towards the exercise’s goal.

Frequently, participants will struggle to overcome their impulse to speak, requiring reminders from the facilitator. Silence is particularly important at the beginning of the exercise, when grouping strategies and frameworks are just starting to emerge. Later in the activity, once firm clusters have been established and the participants are laboring to categorize straggler sticky notes, I tend to relax the policy of silence and allow for some productive conversation. However, always be on the lookout for dominating behaviors, and reinforce silence if necessary to combat them.

The deceptively simple mechanics of affinity diagramming can falsely lead to the conclusion that this activity is easy and foolproof. In reality, it is an advanced method that requires a skilled facilitator and conscientious participants. By providing a clear focus question and guarding against keyword matching and groupthink, you can sidestep some of the most common pitfalls associated with the method.
