<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>To ensure activity participants get the most out of UX activities, use a three-step process to conduct them: explain, execute, and examine.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Collecting knowledge and ideating on design solutions are activities best done collaboratively. This teamwork collaboration is often facilitated or led by UX professionals, in the form of <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/facilitating-design-studio-workshop/">workshops</a> for <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ideation-in-practice/">ideation</a>, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prioritization-matrices/">prioritization of ideas</a>, <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/affinity-diagram/">affinity diagramming</a>, or creating <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-mapping-cheat-sheet/">empathy maps</a> (and numerous other <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-thinking-team-building/">design artifacts</a>). </p>

<p>To avoid a "vanity" workshop and maximize the likelihood that your UX activities yield useful insights, use the following <strong>3-step model</strong> to facilitate UX activities: <strong>explain</strong>, <strong>execute</strong>, and <strong>examine</strong>.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-overview-1">Overview</h2>

<div style="text-align:center">

<figure class="caption" style="display:inline-block"><img alt="The 3-Step Model overview: Explain, Execute, Examine" height="626" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2018/06/28/screen-shot-2018-06-28-at-90212-am.png" width="800"/>

<figcaption><em>The 3 E's model ensures your group work takes place quickly, efficiently, and with maximum impact. </em></figcaption>

</figure>

</div>

<p>Effective groupwork facilitation means that participants have enough guidance to move quickly and effectively through activities, while having the agency and freedom to produce actionable new insights and ideas to move projects forward. A workshop facilitator* can help set a team up for success by doing the following steps for every UX activity:</p>

<ol>

<li><strong>Explain</strong> refers to how the activity or workshop is set-up and introduced to participants.</li>

<li><strong>Execute</strong> pertains to the actual activities and the faciltator's role during this groupwork.</li>

<li><strong>Examine</strong> relates to the "wrap-up" and conclusion of the activity, with presentations, reflections, and open questions.</li>

</ol>

<p><em>* Note that the terms "facilitator" and "participant" are used with a slightly different meaning in the context workshops compared to the context of a usability study. A usability study “facilitator” is the usability specialist running the study and the “participants” are the test users. In this article, the workshop “facilitator” is the UX team member in charge of a specific team activity, where the “participants” are other UX people and/or stakeholders.</em></p>

<h2 id="toc-step-1-explain-2">Step 1: Explain</h2>

<p>Activities should always begin with brief introduction and explanation. This can be kept brief (in the nature of a summary) if all team members have participated in many previous activities of the current type, or if they all participated in the same type of activity very recently. If some participants are new or if the last similar activity was long ago, more detailed explanations are needed.</p>

<ol>

<li>

<p><strong>Introduce the activity.</strong></p>

</li>

<li>

<p><strong>Describe how to do the activity.</strong></p>

<ul>

<li>

<p>Walk through the process, identifying the steps needed to complete it.</p>

</li>

<li>

<p>When applicable, define roles for different group members and explain who should do what. The roles can be specific to the activity at hand or can reflect participants’ expertise, with the goal to bring a particular perspective or knowledge to the activity.</p>

</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>

<p><strong>Discuss context.</strong></p>

<ul>

<li>

<p>Why it is important?</p>

</li>

<li>

<p>If applicable, educate participants by generalizing and explaining other types of goals the activity is good for. Think about contexts outside of the workshop:</p>

<ul>

<li>

<p>Does the activity lend itself well to specific scenarios?</p>

</li>

<li>

<p>How could individual participants apply the same process elsewhere?</p>

</li>

</ul>

</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>

<p><strong>Answer any clarifying questions</strong></p>

</li>

</ol>

<p style="margin-left:40px">During this setup, aim to describe the general characteristics of the activity, but never state what will be learned as a result of taking part in the activity. As a facilitator, you are responsible for establishing a neutral, positive foundation prior to the execution of the exercise.  Like in data-collection activities, you should <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/talking-to-users/">avoid biasing statements or questions</a> that can influence participants.</p>

<h2 id="toc-step-2-execute-3">Step 2: Execute</h2>

<p>Once the exercise is explained, let participants carry out what you’ve asked them to do. Wander around and help individuals or groups.</p>

<p>Your goal is to remain out of focus as the activity goes on, unless you were assigned a specific role during the activity. If something goes wrong (for example, participants appear lost or do not obey the rules of the activity), you may step in to review the information from the explanation phase. Otherwise, your role should be to observe and listen for comments that can be pointed out during the postexercise discussion.</p>

<p>Remember that, as a facilitator, you are not meant to be the sole expert. Rather, it is your responsibility to define the logistics for how participants will teach, learn, and contribute. If you are  asked an outcome-specific question, reframe the question and ask it back to the team.</p>

<p>For example, if asked Is this the right direction? reply:</p>

<ul>

<li><em>Does the whole team feel this is the right direction?</em></li>

<li><em>Did you consider other directions then choose this one? Why?</em></li>

<li><em>What led you here?</em></li>

<li><em>What makes you doubt this direction?</em></li>

</ul>

<p>Throughout the execution phase, track and vocally state time to the room. A general rule of thumb is to announce the halfway mark, the 5-minute mark, and a 1-minute heads up.</p>

<h2 id="toc-step-3-examine-4">Step 3: Examine</h2>

<p>Once the activity is done, you’ll examine the outcomes as a group. This debrief is split into three parts: present, reflect, and connect.</p>

<h3><strong>Present</strong></h3>

<p>Let participants showcase their work to each other or the group. Predetermine the order and length of time individuals or teams presents their outcome(s). Then, ask questions that deepen everyone’s understanding of their ideas. Though these questions can be initiated by the facilitator, aim to have others from the room ask questions as well.</p>

<p>As a facilitator, don’t be afraid of silence. Silence is often needed so people can think and formulate their comments or questions. Participants are more likely to share if there is a pause in the room (humans naturally dislike silence in conversations and will attempt to fill it).</p>

<h3><strong>Reflect</strong></h3>

<p>Synthesize what was learned from the activity by asking participants a series of reflective questions:</p>

<ul>

<li><em>What did you get out of it?</em></li>

<li><em>Where and how did you get stuck?</em></li>

<li><em>What was fun/hard/frustrating?</em></li>

<li><em>What did you learn?</em></li>

</ul>

<p>Encourage participants to describe the exercise from their own point of view. If there were multiple roles in the activity, ask participants to explain what happened from the viewpoint of each role. Allowing the participants to reflect on and discuss the activity helps clear the excitement of the activity and lets them start the realization process.</p>

<p>If you have trouble getting participants to open up, repeat back some of the comments you heard during the activity:</p>

<ul>

<li><em>Did anyone else in the room experience this? How did you overcome it?</em></li>

<li><em>What surprises you about (output A) vs. (output B)?</em></li>

<li><em>What conclusions can we draw from this?</em></li>

</ul>

<h3><strong>Connect</strong></h3>

<p>Connect the output of the activity to what was previously learned and what’s left to learn.  Synthesize the outputs, especially if they will be used in subsequent activities.</p>

<h2 id="toc-practical-tips-to-improve-facilitation-5">Practical Tips to Improve Facilitation</h2>

<p><strong>Scale this model depending on your constraints.</strong> The above three steps can be done quickly throughout a 30-minute activity (start to finish), or could be expanded for a large, in-depth day-long workshop. If it’s your first time facilitating a group through an exercise, start small with a concrete scope.</p>

<p><strong>Don’t facilitate and contribute at the same time.</strong> It is hard to play both roles well. Because the facilitators act as authority figures throughout the exercise, their contributions will be taken at a high regard (which defeats the purpose of many of our UX collaborative exercises). Better to stay neutral. If you do not have the luxury of a dedicated facilitator, try rotating facilitators for each activity. The benefit here is twofold: (1) you avoid recurring bias, and (2) you build up a facilitation competency within a team.</p>

<p><strong>Iterate and improve this model after practice.</strong> Just as we iterate on our end products, we should iterate on our own processes. After guiding a group through an activity, document what went well and what didn’t. Where did participants ask you the most questions? Where did you find it hardest not to insert your bias? These insights will help you improve your practice over time.</p>

<p><strong>Observe other facilitators.</strong> The quickest way to learn as a facilitator is to experience other facilitators. Everyone has a unique style. Observe others and analyze on how they present, execute, and reflect on an activity. A skilled facilitator has a framework that he or she feels confident adapting to any exercise.</p>
