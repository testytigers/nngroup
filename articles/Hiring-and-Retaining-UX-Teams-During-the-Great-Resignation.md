# Hiring and Retaining UX Teams During the Great Resignation

TheGreat Resignationis sweeping through the country. People have been resigning from their positions in record numbers since the spring of 2021. How has this historic phenomenon affected UX teams? What are they doing to evaluate and hire new talent, and how are they trying to keep their existing talent?

We surveyed 676 UX professionals and asked questions about their role, UX team, and approaches to hiring and retaining employees. Besides multiple-choice questions, the survey included anopen-ended questionon what works well and doesn't with hiring and retention for their UX team. Participants shared 305 comments; themes across these were identified usingthematic analysis.

Data was collected using a digital survey in February 2022. The 676 survey respondents had these attributes:

To provide context on hiring activity, we asked respondents to estimate the number of team members, including themselves, who report directly to their boss (if the respondent was not a manager) or report to them (if the respondent was a manager). The average UX-team size reported by all respondents was 9 (but some large estimates skewed the data). The median UX-team size was 6 and the mode was 5.

Over the past year, 42% of respondents said they had participated as an interviewer for 1–2 open positions, 32% said they had helped with 3–4 open positions, and24% said they had helped with 5 or more. These numbers suggest very active hiring activity for these UX professionals.

We also asked respondents to estimate their satisfaction with the following hiring and retention topics on a5-pointscale ranging from very dissatisfied to very satisfied:

The survey displayed these topics in randomized order to reduce bias.

Among the different topics, confidence in hiring recommendations got the highest level of satisfaction (it was significantly higher than satisfaction with all other topics,p<0.05). Participants reported mild satisfaction (over the neutral point of 2 but less than 3,p< 0.05) with filtering out unqualified applicants, hiring qualified applicants, retaining high-performing employees, and team diversity. Training on hiring practices was the only topic that participants were dissatisfied with (less than 2,p<0.05).

Why are these UX professionals satisfied with their hiring recommendations despite lackluster training? One possible explanation that's consistent with psychology research is overconfidence. Research by Kausel, Culbertson, and Madrid suggests that information gathered fromunstructured hiring interviews— the predominant hiring interview approach — breedsoverconfidence and unfounded risk taking. (Daniel Kahneman describes the cognitive bias of overconfidence as the "illusion of understanding.”)

In general, even though there was a slight tendency for managers to be more satisfied with the hiring and retention topics than nonmanagers, these differences were not statistically significant, with one notable exception:nonmanagers were more dissatisfied than managers with retaining high performing employees(p<0.05). It's possible that managers were more invested in the hiring and retention process and thus may be more prone to aconfirmation bias.

There are remedies to this problem, however.Incorporating structure into UX hiring interviewsis the best way to reduce the pernicious effects ofcognitive biaseson ourdecision making. But how much structure is currently being used?

To gauge the use of structure by UX teams in their hiring process, we asked respondents to select from a list of 10 structuring tactics. These tactics were chosen from a meta-analysis of hiring-interview research conducted by Levashina, Hartwell, Morgeson, and Campion. They identified the most popular tactics used in structured hiring interviews. Using more structuring tactics is generally better, but it's still an open research question whether some tactics are superior to others or whether there is an optimal number of tactics to use.

Participants were asked to select all tactics that best described how they interviewed job applicants for their UX teams. The survey displayed these tactics in a randomized order to reduce bias:

The average number of structuring tactics reported by our respondents was 3.91 (95% confidence interval from 3.69 to 4.13). We could not distinguish between respondents who skipped the survey question and those who use no structuring tactics, so this average may be slightly underestimated. The top three most selected tactics were using multiple interviewers, note taking (UX professionalstake a lot of notes, after all), and asking behavioral questions. Situational-interview questions are harder to develop than behavioral questions, which may explain why they were used less often. The popularity of these tactics suggests UX professionals prioritize feedback from multiple sources and are familiar with the concept ofbehavioral consistency(that recent past behaviors are the best predictor of future behavior for similar situations).

About half of the respondents reported using the same questions and the same interviewers. This is good news. Both these structuring tactics drive consistency in applicant evaluations and improve decision making. However, successfully implementing these tactics requires careful planning, scheduling, and prioritization before interviews occur. For example, for teams to use the same interviewers successfully across several candidates, coordination with people's work schedules is necessary — you can't just substitute an interviewer with someone else who happens to be available. Likewise, using consistent questions across candidates requires the interviewer to plan what they're going to ask, ideally based on an analysis of productive behaviors and skills for the job (which, unfortunately, less than half of the respondents reported using). This type of work also needs to be done before starting the interviewing process.

The least popular tactics were using a scoring rubric, numerically rating candidate answers, or receiving training on hiring techniques. This finding suggests that most hiring decisions are still made based on intuition and that people do not realize thatmechanical scoring can make a difference in the quality of their decisions.

When it came to retaining team members, many respondents echoed the importance of the 3 psychological pillars that form theself-determination theory: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

Autonomy.Participants stressed that it's crucial to assign team members tasks and allow them to exercise their judgment when completing them.

What works well in retaining employees - giving my team ownership of specific tasks. When they feel it's something they own, even when it's difficult, they often pull through knowing that it's directly attributed to their own effort.

Competence. Respondents mentioned that challenges promote their team members’ growth and invest in their career development.

Relatedness.Survey respondents emphasized the importance of a trusting and safe, yet professional relationship between managers and team members.

Our team encourages vulnerability, trust, and care. Because of these operating principles, we have been able to hire and retain talent despite big shifts in the organization.

Participants frequently cited a lack of planning to identify team needs and ideal candidate qualifications before starting the hiring process. A few respondents mentioned they had no idea for which role they were evaluating candidates! In some cases, management had alow-UX-maturityperspective on UX roles, thus making recruiting and hiring difficult. Focusing heavily on UI-design skills when hiring a UX designer is one example of a narrow perspective.

Multiple respondents emphasized determining a candidate's interpersonal skills and compatibility with the UX team and the organization. There was a willingness to sacrifice experience expectations for cultural compatibility. Comments were unclear on how culture fit was determined aside from asking interview questions, and a few respondents cautioned that emphasizing it could lead to homogenous and less diverse teams.

Unsurprisingly, lowUX maturitywas corrosive to UX-talent retention. Some cited a lack of broad company support for UX, such as low UX budget, lack of leadership focus on UX, and little recognition of UX as a reason for retention problems.

Aside from interviews, portfolios were the most frequently cited assessment method. They were also the most controversial assessment method, with some respondents relating their struggles discerning the candidate's specific contributions or process on team projects. Exercises, either in take-home or whiteboard form, were cited frequently as practical demonstrations of a candidate's abilities. Several respondents acknowledged the tradeoff between the additional stress of a whiteboarding exercise versus the "homework" burden of a take-home exercise. Design critiques or presentations were also mentioned but less frequently than these other methods. Participants rarely referenced resumes.

Many respondents shared that they do not have enough qualified UX candidates and that senior UX talent is scarce. A few respondents reported "settling" for junior candidates instead. Recruiters (either an HR department or an external agency) were often cited as unhelpful and uninformed about UX roles. Some respondents expressed a desire for more-diverse job-applicant pools.

A final piece of obvious advice for UX leaders: shift your company to a higher level of UX maturity to attract and keep the best UX staff. Any UX manager worth their salt should already be doing this, which is to say that delays in elevating maturity usually stem from organizational resistance rather than from lack of trying. Still — try harder! Far better to proactively invest your energy and resources inleveling up UX maturitythan worrying about who may leave your UX team next.
