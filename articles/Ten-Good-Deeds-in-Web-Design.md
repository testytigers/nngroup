<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Ten design elements that would increase the usability of virtually all websites if only they were employed more widely.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>When analyzing Web design, it is easy to identify a large number of mistakes that <em> reduce </em> usability:</p>

<ul>

<li>the original top-ten <a class="old" href="/articles/original-top-ten-mistakes-in-web-design/" title="Alertbox May 1996"> mistakes of Web design</a></li>

<li>the top-ten <a class="old" href="/articles/the-top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-1999/" title="Alertbox May 1999"> <em> new </em> mistakes of Web design</a></li>

<li>the top-ten <a class="old" href="/articles/top-10-mistakes-of-web-management/" title="Alertbox June 1997"> mistakes of Web project management</a></li>

</ul>

<p>It is much harder to say what <em> good </em> things to do since I have never seen a website that was truly stellar with respect to usability. The best major site was probably amazon.com as of late 1998, but during 1999 Amazon declined in usability due to the strategy of blurring the site's focus.</p>

<p>Of course, articles that list 30 mistakes can be seen as constructive criticism and a prescription for 30 things to do in a Web project: <em> design to avoid each of the mistakes!</em></p>

<p>Here's a list of ten additional design elements that will increase the usability of virtually all sites:</p>

<ol>

<li>Place your <strong> name and logo </strong> on every page and make the logo a link to the home page (except on the home page itself, where the logo should not be a link: never have a link that points right back to the current page).</li>

<li>Provide <strong> <a class="old" href="/articles/search-and-you-may-find/" title="Alertbox July 1997: Search usability"> search</a> </strong> if the site has more than 100 pages.</li>

<li>Write straightforward and simple <strong> <a class="old" href="/articles/microcontent-how-to-write-headlines-page-titles-and-subject-lines/" title="Alertbox Sept. 1998:Microcontent: writing headlines, page titles, and email subject lines"> headlines and page titles</a> </strong> that clearly explain what the page is about and that will make sense when read out-of-context in a search engine results listing.</li>

<li>Structure the page to <strong> facilitate scanning </strong> and help users ignore large chunks of the page in a single glance: for example, use grouping and subheadings to break a long list into several smaller units.</li>

<li>Instead of cramming everything about a product or topic into a single, infinite page, use <strong> hypertext to structure the content space </strong> into a starting page that provides an overview and several secondary pages that each focus on a specific topic. The goal is to allow users to avoid wasting time on those subtopics that don't concern them.</li>

<li>Use <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/"><strong> product photos</strong></a>, but avoid cluttered and bloated product family pages with lots of photos. Instead have a small photo on each of the individual product pages and link the photo to one or more bigger ones that show as much detail as users need. This varies depending on type of product. Some products may even need zoomable or rotatable photos, but reserve all such advanced features for the secondary pages. The primary product page must be fast and should be limited to a thumbnail shot.</li>

<li>Use <strong> relevance-enhanced <a class="old" href="/articles/marginalia-of-web-design/" title="Alertbox Nov. 1996: Marginalia of Web Design"> image reduction</a> </strong> when preparing small photos and images: instead of simply resizing the original image to a tiny and unreadable thumbnail, zoom in on the most relevant detail and use a combination of cropping and resizing.</li>

<li>Use <strong> <a class="old" href="/articles/using-link-titles-to-help-users-predict-where-they-are-going/" title="Alertbox Jan. 1998"> link titles</a> </strong> to provide users with a preview of where each link will take them, <em> before </em> they have clicked on it.</li>

<li>Ensure that all important pages are <a class="old" href="/articles/disabled-accessibility-the-pragmatic-approach/" title="Alertbox June 1999: Disabled accessibility: the pragmatic approach"> <strong> accessible for users with disabilities</strong></a>, especially blind users.</li>

<li><strong>Do the same as everybody else</strong>: if most big websites do something in a certain way, then follow along since users will expect things to work the same on your site. <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/videos/jakobs-law-internet-ux/" title="2 min. video w. Jakob Nielsen discussing Jakob's Law of Internet UX">Remember <strong> Jakob's Law of the Web User Experience</strong></a>: users spend most of their time on <em> other </em> sites, so that's where they form their expectations for how the Web works.</li>

</ol>

<p>Finally, always <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/tips/usertest/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: 230 tips and tricks for a better usability test, 41 pages"> test your design</a> with real users as a reality check. People do things in odd and unexpected ways, so even the most carefully planned project will learn from usability testing.</p>
