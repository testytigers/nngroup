Nowadays, if you’re walking into a restaurant in Beijing during the lunchtime rush hours, most likely no waiter with a menu would show up to take your order. Instead, a server at the door would greet customers and remind them to order by scanning the QR code on their table. With the online-ordering system, customers can use their smartphones to easily complete actions like placing an order, making special requests, and paying and getting a receipt. Some restaurants are even using serving robots or tracks, thus making restaurant service fully unmanned.

Unmanned restaurants are popular not only in Beijing. According to a report published by Nayuki, a popular milk-tea chain with more than 800 stores all over China, in 2020, 71% of their in-store orders were placed via WeChat and Alipay mini programs. The widespread adoption of unmanned restaurant systems has been made possible by the broad use ofWeChat mini programsand mobile payments among Chinese users.

In recent years, more and more restaurants have developed their own online self-ordering systems using WeChat mini programs, which are services within the WeChat application that do not need to be downloaded or installed to be used. Users must open the WeChat or Alipay app to use these mini programs. In restaurants offering self-ordering services, customers are instructed toscan the QR codeon their table and use the WeChat mini program to order. In addition, after finishing their meals, customers can pay directly in the mini program using WeChat Pay or Alipay. (This integration of two otherwise separate features shows the benefits of thesuper-app approachpopular in China.)

The unmanned-restaurant trend is also present in the US, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, but to a lesser extent. Many restaurants now allow customers to see the menu by scanning a QR code. In some restaurants, people can directly order using pretty much the same interface that they would use at home, if they were to order takeout.

Still, less human help does not necessarily translate into a good user experience.

The degree of human help provided within restaurants varies. Most restaurants (but not all) that use unmanned facilities are fast-food restaurants, aiming to improve dining efficiency.

Some restaurants are completely free of waiters; others might support online ordering in store (for example, by providing a QR code or giving customers iPads), while still relying on waiters to do at least one of the typical restaurant activities: confirming the order, serving the food, taking the check to the customers, and helping them pay. At a minimum, many restaurants will have abandoned paper menus for sanitary reasons, in light of the COVID-19 pandemic.

However, human help is still present. Even some of the restaurants that are mostly unmanned may still have waiters around to support the elderly or others who have difficulty with the online interface.

In November 2021, we went to Yoshinoya, an unmanned fast-food restaurant in Beijing, to sample the experience of eating in an unmanned restaurant.

Yoshinoya offered mostly unmanned services: customers could browse the menu, place orders, pay and get receipts on their mobile phones; an automatic mini train was responsible for delivering the food. There were still a few waiters in the restaurant to clear tables and address any problems.

After the customer was seated, a waiter would instruct them to scan the QR code posted in front of their seats to access Yoshinoya's WeChat mini program for online ordering.

Upon users tapped thePaybutton, the online system took them to a WeChat Pay page. (Since WeChat mini programs only support WeChat Pay, customers who needed to use a different payment method such as Alipay, Union Pay, or credit card had to ask the waiter for help.)

After confirming the payment amount and bank card used for the transaction, users completed the payment.

Upon payment completion, a confirmation page showed the table number (or the order number for takeout orders) and a detailed receipt.

When the meal was about to be delivered, the customer received a meal pickup notification in their WeChat. The mini train stopped in front of the customer who had ordered the food and would not leave until the customer picked up the tray.

Still, unmanned restaurants are far from perfect. Based on the interview data and our observations, issues fell into two categories: problems with the online system and irreplaceable human services.

Still, some restaurants handled this situation successfully. In Coucou, a hotpot restaurant, several people at the same table could scan the same QR code and order at the same time. As one person added a dish, a message was sent to the other people at the same table. The message showed that person’s WeChat avatar and nickname, followed by the dish that they ordered. On the checkout page, users could also review who ordered each dish.

Users like the convenience of unmanned restaurants and the lack of social interaction, especially when they want to get a quick meal. Restaurant owners can also reduce their operating costs by hiring fewer waiters. But the waiters are still indispensable, as they act as an escape hatch for any issues that may arise — whether nonstandard customer needs or failures in the back end.

In other words, unmanned restaurants are not yet truly unmanned. Nevertheless, we can still draw some insights:

Do not ask before you give.People should be given access to the online-ordering service without having to subscribe to the restaurant’s WeChat account. This approach is a deceptive pattern that not only forces users to spend unnecessary time with the interface but also annoys users and ultimately diminishes trust in the brand. The secret to getting quality subscriptions is making customers happy by providing a great service and a great experience.
