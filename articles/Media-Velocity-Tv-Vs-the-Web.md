<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>The granularity of user decisions is much finer on the Web, which is dominated by the instant gratification of the user&#x27;s needs in any given instant. Content must cater to this rapid pace.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>I've been reading <a href="http://www.amazon.com/dp/014243714X?tag=useitcomusablein" title="Amazon.com: info about English translation of the book"> The Tale of Genji</a> — the masterpiece of Japanese literature that was written a thousand years ago and is widely considered the world's first novel. I hope I don't offend my Japanese friends by saying that I find the action pretty slow moving. It's a wonderful book, but Lady Murasaki spends many pages to advance the plot even the tiniest bit.</p>

<p>From a historical perspective, it's clear why <cite> The Tale of Genji </cite> racked up the page count. The ladies of the Japanese court <strong> had nothing else to read</strong>. After all, this was the only novel in existence in the entire world. It's not as if Lady Murasaki had to compete with Stephen King or Tom Clancy.</p>

<p>Today, of course, we're in the opposite scenario: everything we write competes with trillions of Web pages, all a few clicks away. As a result, most people actually <a class="old" href="/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="Alertbox: How Little Do Users Read?"> read very few words</a> on the Web.</p>

<p>The <strong> velocity of media consumption </strong> has increased dramatically. Readers no longer linger over lovingly described passages detailing a lord's style of dress. They click here, they click there, they click everywhere. But they don't stay.</p>

<p>People's consumption of print media is different than their use of websites, leading to the many differences in <a class="old" href="/articles/differences-between-print-design-and-web-design/" title="Alertbox: Differences Between Print Design and Web Design"> designing for print versus the Web</a>.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-dimensions-of-media-difference-1">Dimensions of Media Difference</h2>

<p>Of course, I'm simplifying when I say that TV has easy usability and no features. One look at a remote control implies otherwise — and <a class="old" href="/articles/remote-control-anarchy/" title="Alertbox: Remote Control Anarchy"> usability of remote controls</a> hasn't improved since I analyzed it 5 years ago. Indeed, people basically don't use any of TV's advanced features precisely because they're so cumbersome. As a result, the received user experience is pretty simple: turn it on, sit back, and enjoy the show.</p>

<p>The Web has powerful features, is nonlinear, and is user-driven — able to <strong> instantly gratifying </strong> users' current needs based on their search engine queries. <a class="old" href="/articles/mobile-usability-update/" title="Alertbox: Mobile Usability"> Mobile Web use</a> lives even more in the current moment.</p>

<p>Compared to TV, the Web also has a much finer <strong> granularity of user control</strong>:</p>

<ul>

<li>When watching <strong> TV</strong>, you make one decision every <strong> 30–120 minutes</strong>: pick a show or movie to watch, and then it's lean-back time. <em> Ah, easy. </em></li>

<li>When surfing the <strong> Web</strong>, you make a decision every <strong> 10–120 seconds</strong>: leave or stay on this page; leave or stay on this site. Where to click now? Where to click next? <em> Stressful. </em></li>

</ul>

<p>Adding up all these differences explains the <a class="old" href="/articles/powers-of-10-time-scales-in-ux/" title="Alertbox: Powers of 10: Time Scales in User Experience"> fast pace</a> of Web use: the velocity is much higher than we see for TV use.</p>

<h2 id="toc-web-video-an-intermediate-case-2">Web Video: An Intermediate Case</h2>

<p>Video on the Web is an intermediate case between broadcast video (TV) and page-based Web navigation. Preliminary data indicates that most Web videos should be short — typically <strong> 2–10 minutes </strong> — indicating a <strong> usage velocity between Web and TV</strong>, but closer to the Web's velocity of one user decision every 10–120 seconds.</p>

<p>When you develop content, services, and designs for the Web, remember that this medium has a much faster velocity than older media, whether print or TV.</p>
