# Why Organizations Don’t Do User Research and How to Change That

Too many organizations still aren’t doing user researchbefore building new products and features. In the nearly 30 years I’ve been doing research, people still use the same excuses!

Based on my years of experience, plus discussions with many deeply traumatized senior user researchers, I’ve compiled a list of common excuses we get from founders, leaders, and product managers when we suggest that it would be reasonable to do some research.

I’ve been hearing this since the ‘90s. Mostly, I’ve heard it from people who (somehow) did have the time to build the wrong thing first and then hired an expensive consultant (me) to come in and fix it.

This one has several related excuses like “the engineers are waiting on the designs!” Trust me, with the rare exceptions of teams starting from scratch, those engineers have months’ worth of backlogged technical debt they could be fixing instead of building that bad, unresearched, and unwanted feature.

Is it, though?

I do understand why some people feel this way. Spinning up a whole research team from scratch or hiring expensive consultants can be pricey.

The answers to this are very similar to the answers to “We Don’t Have Time!” although there are a couple of extras.

I swear, if I hear thatfake Henry Ford quote about faster horsesone more time, I am going to burn everything to the ground.

The problem isn’t that people don’t know what they want. They often know exactly what they want.Users definitely know what theydon’twant,and the answer to that is generally “whatever thing it is that you’re building without good research.”

The problem is that each user may want something slightly different, and none of them particularly care what the other people want. They are also rarely experts in building whatever product you’re building.

Users don’t know the constraints or care about your business model. They can tell you exactly what problem they want to solve and what they want to accomplish, but they can’t necessarily tell you the best way to do that using your product. Figuring that part out is literally your job! Luckily, good user research can help you understand all those things.

Another flavor of this excuse is “We already gave them exactly what they asked for and they didn’t use it!” I can’t believe we’re still explaining this, but user research isn’t asking people what features they want and then building those in order.

User research is understanding people’s needs, motivations, and problemsand then helping the product team figure out how to build something that satisfies those needs, motivations, and wants. Sure, it’s a little harder than just building what people ask for, but it results in something people actually want.

Now we’re getting into the honest excuses. Hearing that the thing you built sucks is painful. I get it. Do it anyway. In fact, if you talk to people early enough in the process, you’re much less likely to produce something bad in the first place, so you might never have to hear them say terrible things about your darling product.

Sometimes, the thing keeping your organization from learning is the fear of learning something that will hurt somebody’s ego. This is especially true of founder-led companies, where the initial product is the result of somebody’s ✨great idea✨, and they simply refuse to consider that people might disagree with them about how great it is.

Erika Hall, author ofJust Enough Research, put it this way:

“We don’t have time to do research” is usually a synonym for “We can’t risk learning something that takes out a load-bearing myth in our organization.” It’s never actually true.

Before I start making fun of people who use this excuse, I would like to say that I personally findA/B testinguseful in many situations. But it is not a replacement for goodqualitative research. It simply doesn’t do the same thing.

For example, you can’t A/B test a thing until you’ve built it, but you can talk to potential users before you have any sort of product at all. You don’t even need an idea for a product. In fact, talking to potential users is a great way to come up with great ideas for products! It is just so much faster.

An A/B test cannot tell you why the results are what they are. It can certainly tell you whether one thing did better on a certain set of metrics than another thing. That’s pretty much it.

Qualitative research provides all the reasons why certain features or interactions perform well or fail, which means you make better decisions in the future rather than just making wild guesses and then seeing if you were right.

Excuse:"We don't have the budget to change our approach if we find out we are wrong."

My response:I appreciate the honesty here! If you’re not going to change anything, why waste time with research? On the other hand, if you can find out ahead of time that what you’re building is wrong, you can save a lot of time by just giving up entirely.

Excuse:“Doing research means overworking our single overworked UX researcher.”

My response:While I appreciate the concern for the delicate health of my research colleagues, have these organizations considered hiring more researchers? I know budgets are tight, but this is a bad place to cut. For more arguments, see everything I said in the sectionResearch Is Too Expensive.

Excuse:“If we do research, it will cause learned helplessness on the part of the teams.”

My response:I don’t even know how to respond to somebody who would say something like this to another human being. Heaven forbid we make anything slightly easier for the team.

Excuse:"The marketing agency handles all our customers' data, and I don't want to ask them, so I don't step on any toes."

My response:If you can’t step on the marketing agency’s toes, honestly, what are you even paying them for? All joking aside, turf wars between various groups can cause serious problems. Ideally, you want to foster a culture where more people doing various sorts of research is considered a good thing rather than fighting for who gets to control everything.

Excuse:“We have millions of users, so there’s no value in asking for the opinions of just a handful.”

My response:I’d be happy to ask all of them for you, but that level of research might get a little bit too expensive. This is a sad excuse by somebodywho doesn’t understand user research. Or sampling. Or statistics. Or people.

These are all excuses, and not very good ones if you do know anything about user research. It’s very easy to show people why they’re wrong, and sometimes you can even convince them to give good research a try.

Unfortunately, there’s one reason for not doing research that is very common and much harder to overcome.The sad truth is that, in many organizations, the incentives are simply misaligned.

At too many companies, especially larger ones, there is no real benefit to delivering something that really benefits users. Teams are rewarded for building new features, shipping fast, and delivering the latest trends.

By the time something gets rolled out to users and fails to make a positive impact, the people who led the charge have already been promoted or have moved on to build the next big thing that is absolutely going to be a game changer. Trying to convince them to slow down just a bit so that they build something that will be great for users and the business doesn’t work, because they don’t get rewarded for that, nor, in most cases, do they get penalized if the feature fails.

Unfortunately, I don’t have a great recommendation for how to respond to that particular excuse, except maybe to say goodbye.
