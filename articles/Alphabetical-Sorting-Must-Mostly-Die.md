<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Ordinal sequences, logical structuring, time lines, or prioritization by importance or frequency are usually better than A-Z listings for presenting options to users.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Sorting a list of options alphabetically has two main benefits:</p>

<ul>

<li>If users <strong> know the name </strong> of the thing they want, they can usually find it in the list pretty quickly.</li>

<li><strong>Lazy design teams </strong> don't have to work on figuring out a better structure. Because we all know our ABCs anybody can put the items into the correct sequence.</li>

</ul>

<p>The first of these is a true benefit, and alphabetical sorting works fine in some cases. For example, it's usually easy enough to pick out a state from an <strong> alphabetical list of the 50 U.S. states</strong>. Indeed, in this case, an alpha listing is more usable than, say, grouping the states by region or showing them on a map — at least when users need to click only one state (usually their own) to navigate to a page with state-related information.</p>

<p>(Listing states alphabetically is a good choice only when people have to select one state from a menu for navigation or a command. When users need to specify the state as part of their address — as in <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-shopping-carts-checkout-registration/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: E-Commerce User Experience Design Guidelines"> e-commerce checkout forms</a> — it's better to present a text field where people can type the two-letter abbreviation; this is both <a class="old" href="/articles/does-user-annoyance-matter/" title="Alertbox: Does User Annoyance Matter?"> faster and less error-prone</a>.)</p>

<p>Lists of countries and other <strong> known-item </strong> problems are also often fine to alphabetize. However, you do need to ensure that users will know <em> unambiguously </em> the name of their selection. If people have to look at several places in the list, you've defeated the purpose of the A–Z order.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-people-rarely-think-az-1">People Rarely Think A–Z</h2>

<p>For most questions, either</p>

<ul>

<li>users <strong> don't know the name </strong> of the thing they want, making A–Z listings <strong> useless</strong>; or</li>

<li>the items have an <strong> inherent logic </strong> that dictates a different sort order, which makes A–Z listings directly <strong> harmful </strong> because they hide that logic.</li>

</ul>

<p>Here's an example of the latter from our recent testing of whether it's a good idea to <a class="old" href="/articles/copying-a-famous-sites-design/" title="Alertbox: Should You Copy a Famous Site's Design?"> copy the design of successful websites</a>:</p>

<p style="text-align:center"><img alt="Excerpt of screenshot from Zappos.com: a shoe-selling etailer." height="282" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/zappos-alphabetical-sorting.png" width="565"/></p>

<p>The left side of screenshot shows two of the options for narrowing the choices on Zappos <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-ux-homepages-and-category-pages/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Design guidelines for category pages in e-commerce user experience"> category listing</a> of women's shoes. Users can filter by width and heel hight (shown), as well as by style, color, and so on (not shown).</p>

<p>This form of <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/events/tutorials/ia.html" title="Nielsen Norman Group training seminar: Information Architecture 1 - Structuring and Organizing Web-Based Information"> faceted navigation</a> is often useful, but only when users <strong> understand their choices</strong>. In our testing, users rarely employed filtering by width or heel height.</p>

<p>In this case, the order of choices presents major usability problems. It's hard to understand the width widget, and it's even harder to use it to select an appropriate range of widths. Imagine, for example, that you have wide feet and typically prefer shoes in widths EE or 3E. These two (consecutive measures) are at opposite ends of the widget. The same problem occurs if you prefer 1- or 2-inch heels.</p>

<p>A better width sorting would go from the narrowest to the widest: 4A, AAA, AA, ..., 3E, 4E. Few people are likely to understand the "OS" option; it should be spelled out ("One Size") and follow the logical sequence of individual widths.</p>

<p>Similarly, heel heights should be sorted from lowest to highest: Flat, Under 1in, 1in, ..., 5in &amp; over.</p>

<p>Interestingly, after our study, Zappos redesigned their site: heel heights are now presented correctly, but widths are still alphabetized. So, they now have the usability of their faceted navigation half right.</p>

<h2 id="toc-better-sorting-than-az-2">Better Sorting than A–Z</h2>

<p>Widths and heights are <strong> ordinal </strong> data, meaning that they have an inherent monotonically increasing sequence. Such items should almost always be sorted accordingly.</p>

<p>Other times, items have domain-related <strong> logical groupings</strong>. You can often determine this underlying logic in a <a class="old" href="/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/" title="Alertbox: Card Sorting - How Many Users to Test?"> card sorting</a> study where you ask users to group related items together.</p>

<p><strong>Time lines </strong> and <strong> geographical location </strong> are other groupings that are often useful. Finally, you can let the <strong> importance </strong> or <strong> frequency </strong> of use guide how you <a class="old" href="/articles/prioritize-good-content-bubbles-to-the-top/" title="Alertbox: Prioritize - Good Content Bubbles to the Top"> prioritize long listings</a> rather than default to less-useful alphabetical sorting.</p>

<p>Depending on the nature of your information, usability might be better served by yet other types of structures. And yes, in a few cases, this might even be the alphabet. But typically, when you reach for an A–Z structure, you should give yourself a little extra kick and seek out something better.</p>
