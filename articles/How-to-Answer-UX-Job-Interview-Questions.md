You’ve spent countless hours crafting the perfectdesign portfolio. Perhaps you’ve made somerevisions to your resume, too. And your efforts are working! You’re receiving callbacks and getting interviews with UX team members and hiring managers.

But you haven’t landed that UX job yet. Why? Maybe during these interviews, you noticed perplexed looks on your interviewer’s faces. They ask multiple followup questions regarding basic details you thought you had already covered. Your interviewers appear prepared to take notes but write down very little of your answers.

These are clues to invest less time in your written communication and more in your verbal communication. Two celestial acronyms, STAR and METEOR, can help you organize your thoughts and overcome the past behavioral and situational questions frequently used in hiring interviews.

Past behavioral questions ask you torelate a previous experience relevant to the role. These questions are rooted in the principle ofbehavioral consistency— thathow you’ve acted in the past is a reasonably good indicator of how you’ll perform in the future,as you will try to avoid internal cognitive dissonance or conform to external societal pressure.

Situational questions confront you with ahypothetical scenarioand probe your default intentions and responses. These questions emphasizejob knowledgeand help prospective employers evaluate whether your problem-solving approachaligns with their expectations and preferences. Situational questions often pose a dilemma that, to be solved, will requiretradeoffs, working within constraints, crafting plans, awareness of common business goals, or understanding people. Although situational questions and whiteboarding design exercises are not identical, situational questions are often the basis of whiteboarding exercises or case studies where the interviewer assesses your tactical thinking via a contextual challenge.

An example of a situational question:

You recently joined an Agile product team as a UX designer. Over the past several sprints, you’ve been surprised by being given design solutions from the product owner, asked to prototype and improve their visual fidelity, and then hand them off to development. You learn from colleagues that product management and engineering created these designs in meetings you were not invited to attend. What, if anything, would you do?

In ourstudy of how UX teams hire and retain new team members, we found that 80% of respondents reported using behavioral interview questions to evaluate candidates and 63% reported using situational questions.You will likely encounter these questions if you’re applying to UX jobs.

Why are these question types used? Because they’re amongthe most effective tools available for evaluating candidates. A meta-analysis of research by Paul Taylor and Bruce Small found that situational and past behavioral questions are helpful predictors of job performance.

They’re also cheap to develop and easy to administer and — when UX teams define good or poor answers for such questions based on skills they consider critical to the job — they result in less-biased, more-consistent, and more-accurate evaluations from interviewers. Unlike job-knowledge trivia or brain teasers, these questions are perceived as fair (a type ofvaliditycalled “face validity”) by candidates.

Past behavioral questions require you to share an experience. Remember the acronym STAR to give successful answers:

Here’s how you would apply STAR if asked the following past behavioral interview question in an interview:

Tell me about a time where you had to compromise with the development team on a feature’s design. How did you resolve the issue?

What if you don’t have an excellent example to discuss? First, don’t panic! Request a moment to think from your interviewer and ask yourself these questions:

For example, maybe the question asks for an experience where you’ve managed a UX team’s research budget, but you’ve never done that before. You could give answers such as:

“I’ve never managed a UX team’s research budget before, but I did manage a $500,000 budget for a marketing team. Could I talk about how I managed that?

“I’ve never managed a UX team’s research budget before, but I actively manage my household budget. Could I discuss my process for that instead?”

It’s also understandable to want to share only anecdotes portraying you in a positive light. But this tactic reduces your number of shareable stories and does not make you stand out among the many candidates who use it. Consider sharing past experiences where you stumbled and made mistakes, so long as you analyzed, learned, and changed from those experiences.Your ability to convey self-awareness and progress is far more valuable than your ability to convey unrealistic perfection. It’s far easier for a UX manager to coach a new hire on tools and techniques than it is to coach them on personal responsibility, teamwork, or integrating constructive feedback.

Here’s an example of applying METEOR to a situational question in an interview:

Over the past several sprints, you’ve been surprised by being given design solutions from the product owner, asked to prototype and improve their visual fidelity, and then hand them off for development. You learn from colleagues that product management and engineering created these designs in meetings you were not invited to attend. What, if anything, would you do?

Whereas STAR and METEOR help us focus on identifying valuable content,storytellingis how we combine and communicate it effectively to our interviewer.But skillful storytelling does not come naturally. Research by Adrian Bangerter, Paloma Corvalan, and Charlotte Cavin found thatresearch participants who answered past behavioral questions with a storyreceivedbetter hiring recommendationsfrom interviewers. However, their participants used storiesonly 23% of the time!

There is a technique that can help, though. A study by Serene Lin-Stephens and colleagues found thatpracticing, training, and referring to relevant images helped participants become more skillful storytellers during interviews.

Actual interviews are not the best setting for practicing interview skills: you’re unlikely to receive crucial feedback on your interview performance due to legal concerns or the hiring manager’s bandwidth. You can, however, practice storytelling in mock interviews with a friend or colleague.
