In our course onproduct and UX partnerships, people from both UX and product roles often ask, “Who does what betweenUX and product management?” and “How can we better define roles and responsibilities?”

In this article, we outline how to set UX roles and responsibilities throughout product development, while considering other partners’ participation. Because Agile product development is collaborative and iterative, a flexible RACI matrix, also known as a responsibility-assignment matrix, serves as a useful framework for planning how the work will get done.

Role involvement in product development varies by organization and across teams. UX professionals may often find themselves questioning who does what and when to involve other roles such as product management andengineering. The RACI helps teams zoom out from the work to see where other roles should be involved.

Definition:ARACI,also known as aresponsibility-assignment matrix, outlines how individuals with different specializations will participate in tasks such as work phases (e.g.,discovery), activities (e.g.,usability testing), and creation of deliverables (e.g., writing ascreeneror designing aprototype).

Each person or role has its own column and each phase, activity, or deliverable has an individual row. Each cell in the matrix specifies the involvement of the corresponding party with the task. The involvement is specified through one of the four lettersR(Responsible), A(Accountable), C(Consulted), andI(Informed) —hence theacronym RACI.For example, in the RACI above, the product manager is responsible and accountable for the task of defining objectives and key results, whereas the UX/product designer and the engineering teams should be consulted. Overall role involvement at the phase level is based on the type of participation that appears most for activities and deliverables therein.

Even though RACIs are typically used inprojectmanagement (task completion), which is different fromproductmanagement (value delivery), they work well in both contexts to assess whether the right roles are involved and contribute where necessary. The goal in using the RACI isn’t to plan out a big, waterfall-style project, but to support efficient delivery of value to end users.

Assigning a level of involvement to a particular task involves more than looking at job descriptions or titles alone. In general, UX contributors should beResponsibleandAccountablefor phases, activities, and deliverables related to user research and design. UX managers often areConsultedin such activities. Other factors that help determine how a role participates throughout product development include skillsets, relationships with others, historical knowledge of the product or organization, time available, and levels of ambition and interest.

For example, a product manager who understands good interviewing techniques may beResponsibleandAccountablefor user or stakeholder interviews at various points throughout product development. However, in such a case, a UX lead could still be considered eitherResponsible,if they collaborate to conduct the interviews with the product manager, orConsulted,if they give advice on interview questions.

If UX is unable to participate in the interviews due to time constraints, at the very least some UX team members should be marked asInformedin the RACI, to ensure that the product manager shares findings and insights from the interviews, since these will impact UX work.

Roles that areResponsibleandAccountablefor a given task need to work together incollaborative partnershipto get the work done. The accountable person should ensure how and when anyConsultedandInformedroles are brought into the process.

For example, a UX lead who isResponsibleandAccountablefor running discovery research decides when to consult their UX manager and when to speak to stakeholders and leaders about progress. Even though UX can and should collaborate on this task with product and engineering since they’re sharing responsibility, the primary accountability would fall on UX.

Once roles and responsibilities are set, it doesn’t mean you can ignore each other completely until it’s time to review the outputs or launch the design. Check in with each other along the way. Visualizing the work to be done with a RACI prevents handoffs of deliverables from one team to the next and fosters a design-as-process culture instead of design-as-service one.

Taking time to openly discuss what’s happening and who’s doing what in product development enables teams to identify gaps in their processes and avoid common misalignments that impair product outcomes. The RACI can act as training wheels for an open conversation about roles and responsibilities.

As your team establishescommon ground, you might eventually graduate from the RACI. Working together often, over time helps team members understand when and how to involve other roles and makes obvious who should be responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed throughout product development. The collaboration won’t always be perfect but using tools like the RACI can point teams towards improving both partnerships and product outcomes.
