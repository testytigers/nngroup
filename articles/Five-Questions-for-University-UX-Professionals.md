Have you ever attended a university? Almost everyone reading this article will have some experience applying to, attending, or trying to graduate from a higher-education institution of some kind. Hopefully, it was (or is currently) a positive experience overall. However, in many cases, the college journey has plenty of challenges — the first of which is simply applying to the right schools.

The primary target audience of most university websites is still made of prospective students — at both the undergraduate and graduate levels. As we gathered data to update ourreport on good university-website design, many well-known findings describing the experience of prospective university students reemerged. We also discovered new insights reflecting the ever-changing state of higher education — particularly in response to COVID-19.

This article presents five major insights drawn from the many new findings added to the report and focuses on prospective university-students’ experience. Each insight is posed below as a question that UX professionals in higher education might ask themselves to determine whether they have adequately accommodated the corresponding user need in their work.

In recent years, a significant number of graduate programs have begun offering online options. This shift was only accelerated by COVID-19. Many prospective graduate students — especially those pursuing master’s degrees — want to find an online offering that will allow them to continue their school while working or taking care of a family. These prospective students look for part-time and flexible programs that areasynchronousorblended.Many prospective graduate students have a hard time finding clear descriptions, illustrations, and examples of these online offeringsand are frustrated because they cannot accurately picture themselves in these programs.

Program-detail pages are the best place to give prospective graduate students an accurate picture of what it will be like to participate in an online program. Placing this information on program-detail pages allows individual departments to outline what their online programs will look like, because formats often differ across the university. Many universities expend significant resources sending advertisements to prospective students. These advertisements generally describe the school and attempt to persuade the recipient to apply to it. They come both as printed materials in the mail (i.e., brochures, flyers, or information packets) and as emails.

These advertisements are often sent in the fall, when most prospective students are narrowing the list of universities they are considering. Frequently, the schools that make students’ short lists are local schools or schools that friends or family have previously attended — particularly if students are trying to save money (through in-state tuition), stay near family, or haven’t decided what they want to study. In cases where students are not as concerned about cost and have decided what they want to study, schools that are farther away and have strong programs in their subject area are also likely to make it onto the short lists.

In almost every case,students are annoyed by advertisements that come from schools they are not already considering.This is partially because prospective students are overwhelmed by the effort required to read and consider every advertisement they receive. Many students reported receiving multiple advertisements a day — especially as emails. In some cases, the same schools sent multiple advertisements to the same students in a short period of time. In our research,the advertisements that students read and acted on were those that came from the schools they were already considering.

Prospective students often turn to a search engine to find the information they need. Many of the answers to their top questions take the form of numbers, dates, or short phrases, which are easily served up infeatured snippetson search-engine-results pages (SERPs). When the answer they’re looking for is provided by the search engine, prospective students tend to trust it and stop their search (a behavior calledgood abandonment— “good” referring to the positive user experience, not necessarily to what a university would want). Prospective students aregrateful when they don’t need to dig deep into a university websiteto find the information they are looking for.

In the initial phases of school exploration, prospective students are likely to search the name of a school rather than for answers to detailed questions. They expect to see basic information which answers some of their primary questions, such as the top 4 questions referenced above. Students tend to use this information to quickly check if the university is a viable option for them.

The obvious implication of this finding is that universities shouldensure that the information returned in featured snippets on SERPs is accurate.

However, a second implication relates to our first finding, referenced at the beginning of this article. The underlying factor causing prospective students to prefer the information in featured snippets over looking at university websites is their desire to move quickly and efficiently. University websites would be well advised tofollow the pattern demonstrated by SERPs. Surfacing the answers to prospective students’ top questions on the homepage and on search-results pages will prevent them from having to read long passages and scan large amounts of text to find important information. It is also critical that the main navigation options havehigh information scentandguide students to the information they seek, since the answer to every question cannot sit at the top of the homepage.

Prospective students expect the advertisements they receive from universities to bepersonalizedand relevant to their situations.Good personalization can be very persuasive, while poor personalization tends to be harmful. Conclusion

Prospective university students are on a mission: to decide which universities they will apply to. Any efforts to increase the speed and efficiency with which future students can find answers to top questions raises the chances that they will apply to your school. However, prospective students are not easily swayed when they have their minds made up. It is best to focus time and resources on identifying and connecting with those individuals who already have your school on their radar.
