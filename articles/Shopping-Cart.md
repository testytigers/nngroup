<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Many shoppers use the shopping cart to make final purchase decisions. Usable shopping carts provide product detail, allow access to product pages, and let users easily delete items.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>The typical ecommerce site is designed to let shoppers find items, add them to the shopping cart, and proceed through checkout. A nice linear process, you may think. All ready to <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/micro-conversions/">define a "funnel" in your analytics software</a> and be done with any pesky user experience considerations beyond that linearity.</p>

<p>Yet in our <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-user-experience/">ecommerce research with actual users buying on websites</a>, we see many shoppers <strong>use the cart as a holding area</strong>, to collect items they’re considering, but aren’t yet convinced they want or need to buy. The cart often acts like a dressing room in a clothing store — a spot where users consider items and make final decisions. As such, the shopping cart needs to do more than collect information about items of interest. The cart also needs to <strong>assist shoppers in making final decisions</strong> and proceeding to purchase. Here are 4 ways of doing so.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-1-provide-access-to-a-full-shopping-cart-1">1. Provide Access to a Full Shopping Cart</h2>

<p>Providing a page in the checkout flow that is <strong>dedicated to displaying the full cart</strong> helps users make their purchase decisions. They can use the cart to review their selections, check details, compare items, and even finesse totals to meet budgets or <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/e-commerce-usability/">qualify for discounts</a>.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Chicos' Shopping Bag shows the clothing in the right colors, size, style and product numbers, along with price, quantity picker, and subtotal" height="471" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/chicos.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Providing a page for the shopping cart, as is done on Chicos.com, gives the users a chance to focus on the cart before starting the checkout process.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Having a full shopping cart would hardly have been necessary as a usability guideline in the old days where everybody did so, but today we need to tell designers to do so. Some sites only allow users to view the shopping cart in a minicart view that appears as an overlay or as part of the checkout process, but not as a dedicated page. For many users, that approach is fine. However, for shoppers who are trying to consider a purchase or make a decision, this can make decision making more difficult, as the cart is relegated to a small area of the screen.</p>

<p><strong>Minicarts give users quick, but limited, access</strong> to information about what is in the cart. They are typically small and only display a few items at a time. They force the shopper to do more work to see items in the cart, and make comparison between items more complicated, as users need to scroll the small area of the screen.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Hautelook's shopping cart is an overlay that scrolls, remeniscent of a megamenu. Products, prices, and removal controls are shown, but not quantity picker." height="443" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/hautelook_minicart.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Hautelook provided a minicart view of the shopping cart to users who clicked on the </em>Cart<em> icon on the page. This view displayed 2.5 items, requiring scrolling to see any additional items.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Sites that only offer this minimal view of the cart often move users directly to the checkout process when ready to purchase, rather than providing a page with a dedicated view of the items in the shopping cart. The cart, if displayed at all, is shown in only a small section of the checkout page. This can cause the same problems as the minicart and can make comparison more difficult.</p>

<p>The minicart pattern also eliminates the step in the shopping process where users can confirm what items are in the cart and make any edits, selections, or deletions. Editing the cart becomes secondary to proceeding through checkout, and as such, may become a distraction during the checkout process. Users may realize that they have items they no longer want in their cart as they reach order confirmation pages.</p>

<p>Within a checkout process, sites need to keep the focus on purchasing, eliminating distractions that might keep the user from making a purchase. Providing a separate, dedicated shopping cart page can help <strong>keep the user more focused during the checkout process</strong>. The shopping cart is then where the user makes the purchase decision, allowing the user to focus on completing the purchase (rather than editing the cart) in the checkout flow.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Hautelook shows cart to the right of the shipping and payment options, during checkout." height="529" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/hautelook_checkoutprocess.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>There was no </em>Shopping Cart<em> page on Hautelook. Shoppers could only view the minicart or start the checkout process, where a view of the cart was displayed as part of the process.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-2-list-product-details-and-a-clear-product-image-2">2. List Product Details and a Clear Product Image</h2>

<p>Because shoppers are making final purchase decisions in the cart, it is essential that the cart clearly summarizes the items in it. In the cart, include product images that are large enough to show some details, allowing the shopper to differentiate an item from other similar items that he may be considering. Include the product name and any attributes that the user selected, such as size and color, along with the price. Clearly showing these details <strong>helps shoppers remember and compare items in the cart</strong>.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Cabela's cart is surrounded by clutter on its own page. It includes printing controls and recommendation links." height="618" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/cabelas_productdetail.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Cablelas.com listed the name, size, and color of each item next to a clear picture.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/"><strong>The product image is essential</strong></a>. The image helps users who are using the cart to comparison shop. Images also assist users who aren’t comparing items, by reminding them what is in the cart and acting as a quick visual summary. Some sites offer no images and others offer images so small, they might as well not offer them. If a user can’t identify an item, let alone compare an item, based on the image, the image does not add to the usability of the site.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="In Levi's cart, the images in ads were nearly twice as big as the images of products in the cart." height="391" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/levis_smallimages.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Product images in Levi’s shopping cart were so small, it was impossible to identify an item based on its image.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>The image needs to <strong>reflect the users’ selections</strong>. Showing the item in the wrong color or with the wrong options, even if the correct color or options are listed in accompanying text, causes users to doubt the selection is correct.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Boscov's shopping cart is cluttered with discounts and coupons." height="476" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/boscovs_color.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Boscovs.com included product images, but it displayed the products in the wrong colors. The blue shirt shown above is supposed to be dark heather gray, and the red sandals are supposed to be navy blue. This inconsistency causes confusion and makes users doubt the trustworthiness of the site.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-3-link-cart-items-to-full-product-details-3">3. Link Cart Items to Full Product Details</h2>

<p>When shoppers need additional information to make a purchase decision, they want to return to the product page in order to read more information or review product details. As such, it is important that sites provide users with <strong>quick access from the cart to the product detail page</strong>. Linking both the product name and image help users quickly and easily access additional product information.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Although the Gap shopping cart doesn't visually indicate links, product images and names lead to product pages." height="479" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/gap_links.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>From the shopping cart on the Gap’s site, users could click either the product name or the product image to return to the product detail page.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<p>Users need to know that they can click on a product in the cart to see more information. Indicate that product names are links. Many sites rely on a response to a hover to indicate that the product name can be clicked, but this requires that the user guess that the item can be clicked in the first place. Using a <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/guidelines-for-visualizing-links/">different color or text presentation for links</a>, such as the blue text in the example from the Gap shown above, helps users know that they can easily navigate to product information if needed.</p>

<h2 id="toc-4-let-users-easily-remove-items-including-setting-quantity-to-zero-4">4. Let Users Easily Remove Items — Including Setting Quantity to Zero</h2>

<p>While it may seem contrary to the goal of selling more on an ecommerce site, it is essential that a shopper can quickly and easily remove items from the cart. For shoppers using the cart as a holding area, there may be several items in the cart that will ultimately not be purchased. A noticeable, quick, and easy way to remove items works to indicate that <strong>users can easily edit the cart</strong> and also allows them to do so when needed.</p>

<p>A clear link, such as<em> Remove Item</em>, works well for users. Such links clearly indicate what users can do. <em>Delete</em> is slightly less clear for users because it reflects more of a technical view of shopping. They can typically understand it, but shoppers don’t think about deleting items — programmers think about deleting items. <em>Remove</em> is a more familiar term from a user perspective.</p>

<p>Some people also want to set the quantity to zero and update the cart. Many sites prevent users from doing this, either by using a drop-down for quantity that does not include the number zero or by returning an error if the user attempts to make the change. Allow shoppers to remove items by changing the quantity to zero.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="Setting the quantity to zero, which used to work on every website, fails at Adidas with a misspelled and unhelpful red error message, &quot;quantity can not be zero&quot;. The Remove Item link must be used instead, and it's far away from the quantity picker." height="619" loading="lazy" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2014/06/06/adidas_removequantity.png" width="695"/>

<figcaption><em>Adidas.com provided clear links to remove each item. However, users could not set the quantity to zero and update the cart to remove an item.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-supporting-decision-makers-5">Supporting Decision-Makers</h2>

<p>The shopping cart is not the only part of the ecommerce site that needs to support decision-making, but it is a frequently overlooked one. Websites should allow users to store future purchases in their carts too. Some people shop one day and pay a few days later, after they decide. It's not necessary to make saving the cart a user action to support this behavior. Just make the full cart persist, to save customers work and make it easy for them to buy when they return.</p>

<p>Every aspect of a site devoted to selling items is important to the overall user experience and success of the site — our <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce-user-experience/">800+ guidelines for better ecommerce sites</a> exemplify the need to pay attention to many details. The shopping cart must be more than a list of items, by supporting shoppers who use this page to make final purchase decisions.</p>
