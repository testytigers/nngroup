<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>When reading from an iPhone-sized screen, comprehension scores for complex Web content were 48% of desktop monitor scores.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p><strong>UPDATE:</strong> In <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/">our 2016 study</a> assessing reading comprehension on mobile devices, we found that readers can understand short, simple text content on mobile devices just as well as on computers, but they slow down when reading difficult text on mobile. Other factors still make mobile devices more challenging to use, so we still recommend prioritizing brevity in mobile content.</p>

<p> </p>

<p>It's more <a class="old" href="/articles/mobile-web-2009-desktop-web-1998/" title="Alertbox: Mobile Web 2009 = Desktop Web 1998"> painful to use the Web on mobile</a> phones than on desktop computers for many reasons:</p>

<ul>

<li>Slower downloads</li>

<li>No physical keyboard for data entry</li>

<li>No mouse for selection; no mouse buttons to issue commands and access contextual menus (indeed fewer signaling states, as discussed further in our seminar on <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/hci/" title="Nielsen Norman Group full-day training course: From Science to Design - Applying HCI Principles to Real World Problems">User Interface Principles Every Designer Must Know</a>: a touchscreen only signals "finger-down/up," whereas a mouse has hover state in addition to button press/release)</li>

<li>Small screen (often with tiny text)</li>

<li>Websites designed for desktop access instead of following the <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/mobile-website-and-application-usability/" title="Nielsen Norman Group report: Usability of Mobile Websites - 85 Design Guidelines for Improving Access to Web-Based Content and Services Through Mobile Devices"> usability guidelines for mobile </a></li>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/ipad-usability-year-one/" title="Alertbox: iPad Usability - First Findings From User Testing">Whacky app UIs</a> that lack consistency</li>

</ul>

<p><a class="out" href="http://www.igi-global.com/bookstore/article.aspx?TitleId=51657" title="International Journal of Mobile Human Computer Interaction (IJMHCI): *for-pay* access to research paper">New research</a> by R.I. Singh and colleagues from the University of Alberta provides one more reason: it's <strong> much harder to understand complicated information </strong> when you're reading through a peephole.</p>

<p>Singh and colleagues ran a <a class="new" href="/articles/cloze-test-reading-comprehension/" title="Alertbox sidebar: Cloze Test for Reading Comprehension"> Cloze test</a> on the privacy policies of 10 popular websites: eBay, Facebook, Google, Microsoft, Myspace, Orkut, Wikipedia, WindowsLive, Yahoo!, and YouTube.</p>

<p>I did a quick analysis of Facebook's privacy policy, which features:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>5,789 words</strong>, or 35 times the number of words <a class="old" href="/articles/how-little-do-users-read/" title="Alertbox: Risks of Quantitative Studies"> users read during an average page visit</a>.</li>

<li><strong>13th grade reading level</strong>, so only people with a year or more of university would find the text easy to read.</li>

<li><strong>Nicely formatted for Web reading</strong>, including a good use of subheads, bulleted lists, and highlighted keywords, in keeping with <a class="old" href="/topic/writing-web/" title="List of articles about content usability"> guidelines for writing for the Web</a>. (That said, these guidelines also call for short text and an <a class="old" href="/articles/writing-for-lower-literacy-users/" title="Alertbox: Low-Literacy Users - Writing for a Broad Consumer Audience"> 8th-grade reading level</a> when targeting a broad consumer audience and not just Harvard students.)</li>

</ul>

<p>(Last week, Facebook posted a draft of a rewritten privacy policy. The content is now written at an <strong> 11th-grade </strong> reading level, which is a nice improvement. Even better, the endlessly scrolling document has been broken up into multiple pages, with a simple internal navigation system and clear summaries that give users an overview of the information. <strong> Adding structure and navigation </strong> to turn a nightmarishly long, linear document into a tight information space is an example of what I call "<a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/mini-ia-structuring-information/" title="Nielsen Norman Group full-day training course: Information Architecture 1 - Structuring and Organizing Web-Based Information">mini-IA</a>." Well done.)</p>

<p>In any case, there's no doubt that privacy policies count as complicated Web content.</p>

<p>In Singh's study, 50 test participants completed <a class="new" href="/articles/cloze-test-reading-comprehension/" title="Alertbox sidebar: Cloze Test for Reading Comprehension"> Cloze tests</a> while reading the privacy policies on either a desktop-sized screen or an iPhone-sized screen. (The study didn't use an actual iPhone, but since users didn't perform navigation or any interactions other than reading and scrolling, the specific device shouldn't impact the comprehension results.)</p>

<p>Results:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Desktop </strong> screen: <strong> 39.18% </strong> comprehension score</li>

<li><strong>Mobile </strong> screen: <strong> 18.93% </strong> comprehension score</li>

</ul>

<p>Test scores must be <strong> 60% </strong> or higher for a text to be considered easy to understand. Even while reading from a desktop screen, users achieved only 2/3 of the desired comprehension level.</p>

<p>The obvious first conclusion from the study is that major websites have overly complicated content in their privacy policies. Of course, it's not exactly news that privacy policies are incomprehensible. Users know this and usually don't read them.</p>

<p>In our previous research, we have found that users treat "user agreements" and similar site copy with contempt. In approaching such agreement text, users</p>

<ul>

<li>read 10%</li>

<li>scan 17%</li>

<li>skip 73%</li>

</ul>

<p>And this is users' behavior during usability studies, where they know they're being video-recorded. At home, I expect they read even less. Basically, people <strong> click "I agree" without reading </strong> what they're "agreeing" to.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-why-mobile-reading-is-harder-1">Why Mobile Reading Is Harder</h2>

<p>User comprehension scores on the Cloze test were <strong> 48% of the desktop level when using the iPhone-sized screen</strong>. That is, it's roughly twice as hard to understand complicated content when reading on the smaller screen.</p>

<p>Why? In this case, people were reading only a single page of information, and they were shown that page as part of the study without having to find it. Thus, navigation difficulties or other user interface issues cannot explain the increased difficulty. Also, users were tested in a lab, so there were no issues related to walking around with the phone or being disturbed by noises or other environmental events. (In the real world, such distractions and degradations of the user experience further reduce people's ability to understand mobile phone content during true mobile use.)</p>

<p>The only reason mobile scored lower than desktop is the <strong> screen size</strong>, since that was the only difference in the study conditions.</p>

<p>A smaller screen hurts comprehension for two reasons:</p>

<ul>

<li>Users can <strong> see less </strong> at any given time. Thus, users must rely on their <a class="old" href="/articles/short-term-memory-and-web-usability/" title="Alertbox: Risks of Quantitative Studies"> highly fallible memory</a> when trying to understand anything that's not fully explained within the viewable space.

<ul>

<li>Less context = less understanding</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>Users must <strong> move around the page </strong> more, using scrolling to refer to other parts of the content instead of simply glancing at the text. Scrolling introduces 3 problems:

<ul>

<li>It takes more <strong> time</strong>, thus degrading memory.</li>

<li>It <strong> diverts attention </strong> from the problem at hand to the secondary task of locating the required part of the page.</li>

<li>It introduces the new problem of <strong> reacquiring the previous location </strong> on the page.</li>

</ul>

</li>

</ul>

<p>This new research provides striking support for the main conclusion in our <a class="old" href="/articles/mobile-usability-update/" title="Alertbox: Mobile Usability"> usability studies of mobile websites</a>: websites (<a class="old" href="/articles/10-best-intranets-of-2011/" title="Alertbox: 10 Best Intranets of 2011">and intranets</a>) must <strong> deploy a separate mobile design </strong>for optimal usability. Specifically, complicated content should be rewritten to be shorter, with secondary information deferred to subsidiary pages.</p>
