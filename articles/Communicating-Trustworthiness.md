<div class="article-summary article-heading-small-light">

<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>The Web is turning into a low-trust society, hurting the honest sites. Site design can communicate

trustworthiness in several ways, though ultimately the customer&#x27;s actual experience is what matters.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>It is getting hard to trust <em> any </em> website to deliver long-term service without getting very explicit guarantees up front. As the <cite> New York Times </cite> recently wrote, trusting a website "<a href="http://www.nytimes.com/library/tech/99/02/biztech/articles/26web.html" title="NY Times Feb. 1999:

'Caveat Emptor on the Web: Ad and Editorial Lines Blur',

REGISTRATION REQUIRED">is like following a helpful stranger in Morocco who offers to take you to the best rug store</a>."</p>

<p>The current climate on the Web is one of <strong> disregard for customers who are traded like sheep</strong>. Companies buy each other, not to create better products or services, but to take over locked-in users and create more "stickiness." The advice in the <a href="http://www.amazon.com/dp/087584863X?tag=useitcomusablein" title="Book by Shapiro and Varian: 30% discount at Amazon.com"> <cite> Information Rules</cite></a> book is good as far as it goes: Switching costs are indeed an important element in design strategy. But the book can as easily be read as consumer advice: beware of lock-in unless you get sufficient up-front benefits to compensate for the later difficulties.</p>

<p>I am a perfect fit for the eFax service to receive faxes by email. And yet I don't sign up. Why? <strong> Lack of trust</strong>. It would be nice to throw out my old fax machine and get my faxes by email (since I only get faxes when communicating with dinosaurs). But as soon as I put <em> their </em> phone number on my business cards (and thus in thousands of rolodexes), I will be at the mercy of any degradation in service, spam faxing, and who knows what else in the future. I am not accusing eFax of doing anything bad or of having nefarious plans: they are just an example of a useful service that is perceived as scary because of the current trust-impaired environment on the Web. Unfortunately the decent sites suffer from the actions of the devious sites.</p>

<p>History shows that <a href="http://www.amazon.com/dp/0684825252?tag=useitcomusablein" title="Book by Francis Fukuyama:'Trust: The Social Virtues and the Creation of Prosperity'"> high-trust societies fare better than low-trust societies</a>. Right now, the <strong> Web is turning into a low-trust society</strong>, and as long as the main business models rely on advertising and eyeball herding, the situation will only get worse.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-trust-builds-from-the-customer-experience-1">Trust Builds From the Customer Experience</h2>

<p>Studio Archetype and Cheskin Research recently completed a user study of Web trust. The methodology has a few weaknesses (most quotes are from super-users, not regular folks; users were asked to check out sites rather than use them for real) but the main results are credible and match earlier studies.</p>

<p>The key finding is that <strong> trust is a long-term proposition </strong> that builds slowly as people use a site, get good results, and don't feel let down or cheated. In other words, true trust comes from a company's actual behavior towards customers experienced over an extended set of encounters. It's <strong> hard to build and easy to lose</strong>: a single violation of trust can destroy years of slowly accumulated credibility.</p>

<p>This is why it is important to have contingency plans in effect to <a class="new" href="/articles/prepare-trust-demolishing-disasters/" title="Alertbox sidebar: Prepare for Trust-Demolishing Disasters"> immediately mitigate any trust-reducing blunders</a> or scandals.</p>

<p>In the long term, treating customers honestly is the only way to build true, deep trust; but how does one get started on this path? The study found several ways to communicate initial trustworthiness, including some traditional ones like seals of approval (TRUSTe, etc.) and brand reputation.</p>

<p><strong>Appropriate use of technology </strong> from encryption to download speed also matters: users feel that a professionally run server can be trusted more than one that feels like it could break at any time. When customers encounter technical difficulties at a previously preferred site, their reactions (according to a study from 1999) are:</p>

<ul>

<li>29% continued loyalty (user tries again later)</li>

<li>52% split loyalty (found a different site: future use alternates between the original site and the new site)</li>

<li>19% abandon the site forever</li>

</ul>

<h2 id="toc-communicate-trustworthiness-2">Communicate Trustworthiness</h2>

<p>The design itself can communicate trustworthiness in 4 ways:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Design quality</strong>: professional appearance feels solid; clear navigation conveys respect for customers and an implied promise of good service. Typos or difficult navigation communicate disregard for the users.</li>

<li><strong>Up-front disclosure </strong> of all aspects of the customer relationship. For example, <a class="out" href="http://www.nytimes.com/library/tech/99/04/cyber/commerce/05commerce.html" title="New York Times Apr. 1999: 'Internet Retailers Hide True Costs of Purchases'"> reveal shipping charges</a> immediately rather than waiting until after the user has placed an order. You may cheat a few people into ordering by hiding the shipping costs, but many more will abandon the site at an early stage of the process. And those users who do get cheated will only be suckers once.</li>

<li><strong>Comprehensive, correct, and current </strong> content and product selection feel solid. If a site has product photos, it should have good shots of <em> all </em> products. Haphazard, random content signal a brittle service. Rolf Molich and Christian Gram ran usability tests of a map site that did not show a recently constructed bridge: users instantly lost faith in the site and wondered what else was missing from the maps.</li>

<li><strong>Connected to the rest of the Web </strong> with links in and out. Not being afraid to link to other sites is a sign of confidence, and third-party sites are much more credible than anything you can say yourself. Isolated sites feel like they have something to hide.</li>

</ul>

<h3>Sensitive Use of E-Mail</h3>

<p>Users are extremely reluctant to give out their email address because they fear getting endless spam. In self-defense, many users have established secondary email addresses (on services like HotMail) that they can give to less trustworthy companies.</p>

<p>Any time you have a need for capturing a user's email address, it is necessary to provide full disclosure of what will the address be used for. It is also necessary to give users the ability to control how much email they will be getting. For example, it should be possible for a user to place an order and be assured that the only email he or she will receive would be clarifications or confirmations of the order.</p>
