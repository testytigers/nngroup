<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Category and link labels should tell users what will happen when they click on them.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p>Deciding which category or link to click requires cognitive effort. If a page has many links, users must choose the one most likely to help them achieve their goal. This process can be exhausting, especially if the link names are vague and unclear.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-users-prefer-clear-over-clever-1">Users Prefer Clear Over Clever</h2>

<p><a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/link-promise/">Good link names</a> help people quickly and accurately predict what they’ll get before they click. </p>

<p><strong>Descriptive category names that people understand are better than made-up words or internal jargon</strong>. Because website designers are usually domain experts, they’re familiar with internal jargon and sometimes forget that their audiences are not.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for AirBnB. It lists multiple categories of homes including lakes, cabins, tiny homes, and omg!" height="271" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/airbnb.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption><em>Airbnb: Airbnb has a lot of categories for its users to explore. Some of these labels are perfectly descriptive, like </em>Cabins <em>or </em>Tiny homes<em>, while others, like </em>OMG!, <em>are unclear.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h2 id="toc-five-tips-for-good-category-labels-2">Five Tips for Good Category Labels</h2>

<p>Cleaning up unhelpful labels creates a better user experience and can positively impact people’s perceptions of your organization. Use these tips to ensure that your category names are understandable.</p>

<h3>1. Be Descriptive and Relatable</h3>

<p>Your navigation labels should be easily understood, even if they feel boring. Choose function over form, and make sure that your names <strong>are descriptive and that </strong><strong>your audience can relate to them</strong>.</p>

<p>When labels are nondescript, people are more likely to click the wrong link and miss information that they need. In usability studies, we often see users skip a category name with weak <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/">information scent</a>, even though that link leads to the item they are looking for.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for Bon Appetit. It lists recipes, ingredients, cooking, culture, shopping, and the category more. " height="165" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/bonappetit.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption>❌ <em>Bon Appétit: While most of the category names were perfectly fine, </em>Culture <em>was misleading because it covered food news and trends.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for Chewy. It lists dog, cat, more pets, pharmacy, services, give back, Chewy picks, today's deals, and Halloween shop " height="100" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/chewy.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption>✅ <em>Chewy: This pet website clearly labeled</em> <em>its Halloween-related offerings as </em>Halloween Shop<em>. </em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>2. Avoid Made-Up Terms</h3>

<p>Don’t be tempted by the allure of clever names. <strong>Stick to commonly used words.</strong> For example, <em>About Us</em> is better than <em>Company Experienc</em>e because it is a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/">more common phrase</a>.</p>

<p>Featuring <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-centric-language/">user-centric language</a> improves decision time, which is important to website visitors who navigate quickly. If you must use fancy names, always explain their meaning, because people tend to skip over meaningless words.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for Live Nation. It lists, life at LN, biz at LN, work at LN, empowered by LN, and resources." height="110" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/livenation.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption>❌ <em>Live Nation: Made-up category names fit the company’s branding but were hard to understand. </em>Life at LN<em> seemed to be the site’s </em>About<em> page, and </em>Empowered by LN<em> was a list of its charitable efforts. Most users would not guess what these labels led to.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>3. Check for Overlapping Categories</h3>

<p>The most useful categorization schemes <strong>have distinct and descriptive category labels</strong>. They can’t be too broad, narrow, vague, ambiguous, or overlap in meaning.Each of your categories should have a unique identity so users don’t get confused between two similar options. Research methods like <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/">card sorting</a> and <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tree-testing/">tree testing</a> can help you determine the best labels for your categories.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for Delish. It lists, recipes, dinner, summer, kitchen tips, and news." height="48" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/delish.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption>❌ <em>Delish: Most users would have difficulty differentiating between the categories </em>Recipes<em>, </em>Dinners, <em>and</em> Summers<em>. Where would salad recipes be?</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>4. Categorize Based on Users’ Mental Models</h3>

<p>Categories should be <strong>structured based on how your users think</strong>. Users should be able to navigate your website with only their own experiences informing their decision making.</p>

<p>Organizations often categorize components using schemes that are familiar to them. For example, they mirror their organizational charts or arrange products by model number. Instead, research your user base to determine <em>their</em> <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mental-models/">mental models</a>. </p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="A navigation menu for Behr. Under interior paint and primer it lists: Behr Premium Plus, Behr Ultra, Behr Marquee, Behr Dynasty. " height="552" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/17/behr.png" width="945"/>

<figcaption>❌ <em>Behr: While Behr’s categorization mostly followed all our suggested tips, it failed to consider users’ mental models for the </em>Interior Paint &amp; Primer <em>subcategories. People searching for paint are more likely to be interested in what that paint can do for them, rather than what line it is a part of.</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>5. Don’t Rely on Instincts</h3>

<p><strong>Test names with your users</strong> to make sure they can accurately predict which information is contained in each category. <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-research-cheat-sheet/">Research methods</a>, such as card sorting and tree testing, can help you <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/">get feedback on your labels</a> and can show you how to structure your website so that people can navigate it effectively. User research is the only way to guarantee your category names will work for your users. Otherwise, you and your team’s mental models and thought processes could still be tainting your site.</p>

<figure class="caption"><img alt="(Top Image) An old navigation menu for Tide. It lists: shop products, about tide, ingredients, how to wash clothes, washing machine 101, and latest news &amp; coupons. (Bottom Image) A more current navigation menu for Tide. It lists: shop products, our commitment, how to wash clothes, what's new, coupons and rewards, and bag recall program " height="358" loading="lazy" src="https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2024/09/18/tidecombine.png" width="1430"/>

<figcaption>❌ <em>Tide: Tide used to have the category label </em>Washing Machine 101,<em> which overlapped with </em>How to Wash Clothes <em>(top).</em> <em>In a newer version of the site (bottom), </em><em>it kept only the more inclusive </em>How to Wash Clothes<em> (presumably after discovering that the two caused confusion in testing).</em></figcaption>

</figure>

<h3>Conclusion</h3>

<p>Not only do bad category names create confusion and frustration, but they often increase the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/">interaction cost</a> because they force people to guess (often wrongly) what the labels refer to. And that’s going to leave <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/halo-effect/">a bad taste in your users’ mouths</a>.</p>

<p>While communicating a <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/brand-intention-interpretation/">brand identity</a> is important, it should not require sacrificing findability and discoverability. No matter how tempting cool names may seem, good UX suggests staying away from them.</p>
