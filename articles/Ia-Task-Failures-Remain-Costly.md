<span class="article-summary-label">

Summary:&nbsp;

</span>Task success is up substantially compared with usability statistics from 2004. Bad information architecture causes most of the remaining user failures.

</div>

<div class="article-body">

<p><em>"If the customer can't find the product, the customer can't buy it" </em> was one of the first slogans I devised about Web usability. This truism remains in force today, because <strong> getting to the right page </strong> within a website or intranet is the inevitable <strong> prerequisite to getting anything done</strong>.</p>

<p>(When users actually do find what they want, <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/topic/writing-web/" title="List of articles about writing for the Web and how people read online">content usability</a> obviously becomes crucial: people have to understand and like the information at their destination. Still, getting there is the vital first step.)</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-web-usability-metrics-2004-vs-2009-1">Web Usability Metrics: 2004 vs. 2009</h2>

<p>In <strong>2004</strong>, we analyzed the <strong> causes of the user failures </strong> we observed when <strong> user testing 25 websites </strong> for the 1 <sup> st </sup> edition of our <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/fundamentals/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed description of training course">Top Web UX Design Guidelines</a> course. (We've since updated the seminar several times — adding video clips and statistics from newer studies — but, true to the name, most of the top 2004 guidelines were in fact fundamental enough to remain in force today.)</p>

<p><strong>This year</strong>, we conducted a similar broad-ranging <strong> study of 24 websites </strong> for our new <a class="new" href="http://www.nngroup.com/courses/topic/information-architecture/" title="Nielsen Norman Group: detailed description of training course">2-day information architecture (IA) training course</a>. Although there are many other courses on this topic, ours is based on empirical studies of what actually works for real users. Our study's main goal was to develop usability guidelines and get video clips for our presentation, but we also analyzed users' task outcomes, applying the classifications scheme we developed in 2004.</p>

<p>(For the method we used to classify usability problems and score their severity, see our book <a class="old" href="http://www.nngroup.com/books/prioritizing-web-usability/" title="Book by Jakob Nielsen and Hoa Loranger"> <cite> Prioritizing Web Usability</cite></a>.)</p>

<p>The happiest finding was that, averaged across the broad range of sites we tested, the <a class="old" href="/articles/success-rate-the-simplest-usability-metric/" title="Alertbox: Success Rate - The Simplest Usability Metric"> success rate</a> had improved nicely:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>66%</strong> in 2004</li>

<li><strong>81%</strong> in 2009</li>

</ul>

<p>Clearly, Web usability has taken hold in recent years, and Internet managers are getting more reluctant to launch "cool sites" that waste company money and fail to support the business.</p>

<p>Our new study's findings further support task success stats from other recent research, in which we updated previous studies in 3 key areas:</p>

<ul>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/site-map-usability/" title="Alertbox: Site Map Usability">Site maps</a>: success rates improved somewhat, from 69% to 71% in 7 years.</li>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/about-us-information-on-websites/" title="Alertbox: 'About Us' Information on Websites">"About Us"</a>  information: success rates improved moderately, from 70% to 79% in 5 years.</li>

<li><a class="old" href="/articles/store-finders-and-locators/" title="Alertbox">Store finders and locators</a>: success rates improved considerably, from 63% to 96% in 7 years.</li>

</ul>

<p>The amount of the increase obviously varies — from fairly small for site maps, which have recently become a bit of a stepchild for Web designers, to immense for store finders, which are a <em> ca-ching </em> of a money-maker (after all, if customers can't find a shop, they can't go shopping).</p>

<p>Our consulting projects show the same broad pattern: websites are definitely easier to use now than they used to be. This is also <a class="old" href="/articles/intranet-usability-huge-advances/" title="Alertbox: Intranet Usability Shows Huge Advances"> true for intranets</a> — in fact, they've actually improved more than websites because they started out with particularly miserable usability.</p>

<h2 id="toc-ia-problems-still-prevent-task-completion-2">IA Problems Still Prevent Task Completion</h2>

<p>Although usability has improved overall, IA is becoming a sore thumb that's preventing websites from meeting their business goals. The following pie charts show the outcome of user task attempts in our two broad website studies:</p>

<p> </p>

<p style="text-align: center;"><img alt="Two pie charts: 2004 (left) and 2009 (right)" height="250" loading="lazy" src="//media.nngroup.com/media/editor/alertbox/task-outcomes-2004-2009.png" title="Success rate for websites in usability studies conducted 2004 and 2009, showing percentage of users' task failures due to IA problems vs other design mistakes." width="589"/><br/>

<em>Task outcome across usability tests of a broad range of websites: 2004 vs. 2009 results. </em></p>

<p>Yes, success is up. And yes, failures caused by usability problems other than IA are down dramatically. Still, user failures caused by <a class="new" href="/articles/top-10-ia-mistakes/" title="Alertbox: Top-10 Information Architecture Mistakes"> IA mistakes </a>  have shrunk only a bit:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>14% </strong> of task attempts failed due to IA problems in 2004.</li>

<li><strong>10% </strong> of task attempts failed due to IA problems in 2009.</li>

</ul>

<p>Bad IA is now the greatest cause of task failures because it's <em> the </em> stumbling block for getting anywhere on a site. Users try to find their way around a site, and if they're particularly motivated, they might even try again if they fail. But if users are repeatedly led in circles or dumped into no-man's land by weak search, they give up and <strong> leave for another site</strong>. That's why deficiencies in your IA are costing you a lot of money, right now.</p>

<h2 id="toc-beyond-success-3">Beyond Success</h2>

<p>High success rates are nice as far as they go, but they're <strong> only the starting point </strong> for high usability. It's obviously <em> necessary </em> that people be able to use your site. But the mere ability to get things accomplished given enough persistence is not <em> sufficient </em> for users to do business there. They also have to like your site.</p>

<p>We usability people must continue working to eradicate the remaining task failures. But sites must also create a pleasant user experience. To do so, all <a class="old" href="/articles/aspects-of-design-quality/" title="Alertbox: Aspects of Design Quality"> aspects of a site's design must work together</a>; interaction design quality is like a chain that's as strong as its weakest link.</p>

<p>These days, most usability problems that we see in testing don't prevent use of the site, but they certainly <a class="old" href="/articles/does-user-annoyance-matter/" title="Alertbox: Does User Annoyance Matter?"> annoy the users</a>. Sadly, there's still plenty of bad Web design that slows people down, confuses them, or makes information hard to understand. Many such usability problems have literally been documented for more than a decade (see, for example, my list of <a class="old" href="/articles/the-top-ten-web-design-mistakes-of-1999/" title="Alertbox May 1999"> top-10 Web design mistakes of 1999</a>).</p>

<p>People don't leave a website only when it fails them completely. They also leave if it's unpleasant to use. So, even though IA causes only 10% of tasks to fail, you don't get just 10% more business from improving your IA. Our work on the <a class="old" href="/articles/usability-roi-declining-but-still-strong/" title="Alertbox: Usability ROI Declining, But Still Strong"> return on investment (ROI) from usability</a> suggests that business metrics are more likely to increase by around 80%. The extra 70% benefit comes from antagonizing customers less, so that they're more likely to stay on your site.</p>

<p>For most sites, improving the IA should be a top priority (even though it's only the second-biggest <a class="old" href="/articles/non-profit-websites-donations/" title="Alertbox: Donation Usability - Increasing Online Giving to Non-Profits and Charities"> problem for nonprofit sites</a>). Fixing IA problems isn't always easy, but when you consider the business costs of showing customers the door because of bad IA, it's well worth doing.</p>
