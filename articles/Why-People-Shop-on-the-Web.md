<div class="article-body">

<p>A recent study of users who have bought products on the Web has three important findings with implications for Web design:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Convenience and ease of use </strong> are the main reasons people buy at websites</li>

<li>Shoppers only buy 5% of the time they visit e-commerce sites: facilitate product research, cross-shopping, and other <strong> non-buying tasks </strong> that account for 95% of visits if you want to turn people into loyal users</li>

<li>E-commerce is going <strong> international</strong>, with many users buying from foreign sites (this latter conclusion is less true for American <em> customers </em> who mainly buy from domestic sites, but it has profound implications for American <em> vendors </em> who can sell a lot overseas if they bother to serve international customers)</li>

</ul>

<p>The results come from a survey sponsored by the <a class="out" href="http://www.fdih.dk/" title="All content IN DANISH"> Danish E-Commerce Association</a>. The survey was completed by 2,929 Internet users in Denmark, of which 1,780 (61%) had actually bought something on the Web. I am only reporting the answers from this latter group of buying users.</p>

</div><h2 id="toc-ease-of-buying-attracts-customers-1">Ease of Buying Attracts Customers</h2>

<p>Respondents were asked to list the five most important reasons to shop on the Web. Even though low prices definitely do attract customers, pricing was only the third-most important issue for respondents. Most of the answers were related to <strong> making it easy, pleasant, and efficient to buy </strong>.</p>

<p> </p>

<p>Not too surprising, except to note the huge difference in interest between product info and vendor info. On the Web, people are very goal-driven and care more about the specific product they are buying <em> now </em> and less about abstract issues like the characteristics of the company that is supplying that product.</p>

<p>Qualitative research bears this out. For example, the <cite> New York Times </cite> quotes a <a class="out" href="http://www.nytimes.com/library/tech/99/02/cyber/eurobytes/09eurobytes.html" title="Access requires registration"> VP from a site that sells music CDs</a>: <em> "When we ask customers what they want, they don't answer 'community feeling' or 'shopping experience,' they want more music samples, ease of use, speed, and low prices." </em></p>

<p>For this question, "buying" was defined to include purchases on the site as well as purchases in physical stores. Note the difficulty of converting even a motivated shopper into a buyer. The <strong> average outcome is that people don't buy</strong>. This result is even stronger when looking at users' <em> average </em> behavior and not simply their behavior when deliberately looking for product information. When asked why they visited one of the e-commerce sites that sponsored the survey, respondents said:</p>

<p> </p>

<p>Remember, these are people who buy online. And yet <strong> only 5% of their visits to e-commerce sites are to buy</strong>. It is important to support the remaining 95% of customer behavior so that they will turn into loyal users and return to the site when they want to buy.</p>

<p>A site that emphasizes sales too much will turn away users (remember that "no sales pressure" was listed by 39% of shoppers as an important benefit of the Web). Most of the time, users want to research products or find other specific information. So don't despair if your server statistics show many more visits to your pages than actual sales. That's natural and indicates that you are supporting customers' preferred behavior by allowing them to cross-shop and research products before buying.</p>

<p> </p>

<p>Much shopping is obviously local, but there is a good deal of international shopping going on. In particular, American websites are doing brisk business in Denmark and it is interesting to note that the U.S. sells about twice as much as Europe. A clear indication of the <strong> need to improve European e-commerce</strong>.</p>

<p>American e-commerce strategists should not use this survey as an excuse to rest on their laurels, however. Europe <em> will </em> improve and become more of a competitor in the future. Also, Denmark is somewhat atypical in several ways: it is a very small country with a particularly well-educated population, meaning that Danes are probably more likely than many others to shop at American sites. People in bigger countries will demand local service; people with less foreign-language education will be less likely to shop at English-only sites.</p>

<h2 id="toc-related-report-e-commerce-user-experience-4">Related Report: E-Commerce User Experience</h2>

<p>(Update) See also my report with <a href="http://www.nngroup.com/reports/ecommerce/"> 207 design guidelines for e-commerce usability </a> . In usability testing of twenty e-commerce websites reported in this publication, we found that users were only capable of buying in 56% of the cases where they attempted a purchase.</p>

<p>Consider the three numbers discussed here:</p>

<ul>

<li>5% of visitors are interested in buying something during the current visit</li>

<li>About half the people who have an interest in buying attempt to do so during the current visit</li>

<li>56% of the people who attempt to buy are capable of struggling through the website's design to a successful completion of their purchase</li>

</ul>

<p>The result of combining these three numbers is a conversion rate of about 1%, which is indeed a commonly observed number. The biggest long-term potential for increasing the conversion rate would come from motivating visitors to move from lookers to shoppers. However, the Web is notoriously bad at changing users' goals and desires. It is much better at fulfilling existing desires than at creating new ones. Thus, the short-term focus should be on changing shoppers to buyers and on making it possible for users to complete the transaction once they have decided to buy. Fix these two problems and you can quadruple your sales.</p>
